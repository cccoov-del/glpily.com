How to Tell Family or Coworkers You Are Taking a GLP-1—If You Want To

You decide who gets to know about your GLP-1 treatment. Find simple boundary-setting language for family, coworkers and intrusive questions.

♡By GLPily Editorial Team
Oct 11, 2026

Your news, your call ♡

On this page

  1. Decide what you want from the conversation
  2. Words you can borrow
  3. What if someone says the medication is cheating?
  4. Protect personal information

Deciding whether to tell people about GLP-1 treatment is personal. Some people welcome open conversation; others prefer privacy because they have experienced judgment, intrusive questions or unwanted advice. There is no universal rule that you must disclose a prescription to friends, colleagues or relatives who are not involved in your care.

Decide what you want from the conversation

Before sharing, ask yourself whether you want practical support, someone to accompany you to appointments, help remembering a routine, or simply to be open. You can share only the amount of information needed. You do not have to explain your health history, weight, dose or reasons for treatment to satisfy somebody else's curiosity.

Words you can borrow

For a supportive person: “I'm working with my clinician on a health goal, and it would help if you could listen without judgment.” For an intrusive question: “That's personal medical information, and I'd rather not discuss it.” For workplace conversation: “I'm managing a health matter and prefer to keep details private.” Short and calm can be more effective than a long defense.

What if someone says the medication is cheating?

You can choose whether to explain that prescription treatment is a medical decision. You do not have to debate obesity science at a family dinner. In a 2026 interview study of 30 US adults using GLP-1 medicines, participants described stigma that depended on whether the prescription was for diabetes or for weight, and one said they were careful about whom they told. It was a small study, so it can't say how many people face judgment, but it shows that being selective about sharing is a common and reasonable response. (More on this in the stigma behind weight-loss medication.)

Protect personal information

Avoid sharing prescription labels, pharmacy details or account screenshots in public posts unless you intentionally choose to. If a person is part of your care support, agree on what they may know and what stays private. Whatever you decide to tell friends and family, keep your healthcare team fully informed, including other prescribers, your pharmacist and anyone planning surgery or sedation, since GLP-1 medicines can matter for other treatments.

The most useful support is the kind that respects your autonomy. Sharing can be meaningful, and privacy can be equally healthy. You get to choose.
