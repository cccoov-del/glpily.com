This is fiction. It's a made-up sitcom about GLP-1 culture, written by the GLPily editorial team for fun. The characters are invented types, not real people, patients or professionals, and nothing they say is a quote. The joke is aimed at overconfident claims, not at anyone looking for care. When the show mentions a real fact, we've checked it and linked the source at the end.

The show is called Pen Pals. Every episode follows the same rule: it starts with a dramatic claim and ends when someone checks the actual source.

Meet the cast of Pen Pals

Character Catchphrase Superpower Weakness
Dana, the Headline Detective "Says who? Show me the study." Can find a trial's sample size in under 30 seconds Brings footnotes to birthday parties
Marcus, the Spreadsheet Friend "I made a chart." Color-codes literally everything Forgets that people are not rows
The Comment Section (played by a shifting chorus of strangers) "My cousin says..." Unlimited confidence Has never read anything past the headline
Priya, the Pharmacist "Which product, exactly?" Knows where the label lives Has to say "ask your prescriber" 40 times a day
Aunt Bev "Are you allowed to eat that?" Shows up at every holiday Slowly learning to ask "how are you?" instead

The heart of the show is the friendship between Dana and Marcus, who both started a GLP-1 around the same time. Priya works at the pharmacy downstairs. The Comment Section lives inside everyone's phone and sometimes shows up in person, which is as unsettling as it sounds.

The pilot: "The Pen"

COLD OPEN. MARCUS'S APARTMENT. NIGHT.

Marcus is on the couch with three monitors. Dana walks in holding takeout.

DANA: Why are there three screens?

MARCUS: One for my weight trend, one for my water, and one is a chart of my charts.

DANA: That's a cry for help.

MARCUS: It's a dashboard.

A phone buzzes. The Comment Section appears in the doorway: five strangers in matching hoodies.

COMMENT SECTION (in unison): We saw your post. Have you tried taking it twice a week?

DANA: Who let you in?

COMMENT SECTION: You did. When you turned on notifications.

Laugh track. Title card: PEN PALS.

ACT ONE. THE PHARMACY. NEXT DAY.

Marcus bursts in, holding his phone like evidence.

MARCUS: Priya. Emergency. My neighbor ran out of his pen and asked to borrow mine. He said he'd use a fresh needle, so it's fine, right?

PRIYA: (not looking up) No.

MARCUS: You didn't even hear the—

PRIYA: Didn't need to. The label says never share a pen between people, even if the needle is changed. It's a blood-borne infection risk. Also, it's your prescription, not a cup of sugar.

MARCUS: (slowly lowering phone) What do I tell him?

PRIYA: Tell him to call his prescriber or his pharmacy. That's what we're here for.

Aunt Bev walks in, holding a casserole for no reason.

AUNT BEV: Marcus! Are you allowed to be in a pharmacy?

Laugh track.

ACT TWO. DANA'S KITCHEN.

Dana is reading a printed study. Highlighters everywhere. The Comment Section is crowded around the fridge.

COMMENT SECTION: Big news! A study says everyone loses 50 pounds in a month!

DANA: Which study?

COMMENT SECTION: A video about a post about a study.

DANA: (sighing) Okay. Was it randomized? How many people? How long? Did they compare it to placebo? Who paid for it?

COMMENT SECTION: (whispering among themselves) ...The video had really good music.

DANA: That's not a peer-reviewed journal.

The Comment Section slowly backs out of the kitchen like a cat that knocked over a glass.

ACT THREE. MARCUS'S APARTMENT.

Marcus has a new chart. It compares his weight to Dana's. Dana sees it.

DANA: Marcus.

MARCUS: It's just data!

DANA: It's a leaderboard with a fancy font. We're on different medicines, we started three weeks apart, and you put me in red.

MARCUS: ...Red is a strong color.

DANA: Delete it.

He deletes it. Then quietly makes a new chart called "Things We Did This Week": two walks, one cooking night, zero arguments with the Comment Section.

DANA: Okay. That one I like.

TAG. THE PHARMACY. CLOSING TIME.

Priya is locking up. The Comment Section appears, holding a vial with a hand-drawn label.

COMMENT SECTION: We bought this online. The label says "research use only." So we can research it... on ourselves?

PRIYA: (very long pause) No. The FDA has warned that products labeled for research, or "not for human consumption," and sold with dosing tips are of unknown quality and may be harmful. Please don't inject a mystery vial.

COMMENT SECTION: What do we do with it?

PRIYA: Don't use it. If anyone already did and feels unwell, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 or 911 if it's an emergency. Then talk to a real prescriber.

The Comment Section looks at each other. One of them types "she's right" and immediately gets 400 likes.

Why would a GLP-1 sitcom be funny at all?

Because the real situations already are, a little. People are juggling new appetites, family dinners, insurance forms and a phone full of strangers with opinions. Comedy works when it laughs with the person living it and at the overconfidence around them.

Some rules our writers' room follows:

  • Never punch down. The joke is never someone's body, size or decision to get treatment.
  • The expert isn't the villain. Priya isn't a buzzkill. She's the one who saves the day.
  • Facts are checked, even in fiction. If a character states something about a medicine, it matches the label.
  • Nobody gets diagnosed by the Comment Section. Ever.

Pitch your own episode

Want to write episode 7? Here's the formula:

  1. The claim: something dramatic you've heard about GLP-1s.
  2. Who believes it: usually the Comment Section, sometimes Aunt Bev.
  3. The twist: someone checks the real source, like the label, the FDA or the study.
  4. The heart: a small, kind moment between friends.

Just keep it fictional. Please don't turn a real person, their health details or their photos into the plot. And Marcus, if you're reading this, you can track your progress without three monitors. One app with a weight chart, water and a journal will do. (GLPily has those. We had to.)

Common questions

Is this GLP-1 sitcom real?

No. Pen Pals is fiction written by the GLPily editorial team. The characters are invented and nothing they say is a quote from a real person.

Are the medical facts in the sitcom accurate?

Yes, the real facts were checked against official sources. Pens must never be shared, even with a new needle (per the Ozempic and Wegovy labels). The FDA has reported dosing mix-ups with compounded semaglutide and warns about "research use only" products.

Why make jokes about GLP-1 medicines?

Humor can make a stressful topic easier to talk about. The jokes here target overconfident claims and nosy comments, never people's bodies or their choice to get treatment.
