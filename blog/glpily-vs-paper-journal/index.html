Should you track your GLP-1 in an app like GLPily or in a paper journal? Pick whichever one you will actually keep using. A notebook is private, cheap and needs no battery or account. An app is better at reminders, charts and turning months of entries into something you can show your clinician.

We make GLPily, so we are not neutral. That is why this page also lists the places where paper wins, and why it starts with some research that does not flatter apps as much as you might expect.

What does the research say about paper vs electronic tracking?

The best-known head-to-head is the SMART trial, published in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine in 2012. Researchers randomly assigned 210 adults in a behavioral weight-loss program to one of three ways of self-monitoring: a paper diary, a PDA (yes, a pre-smartphone handheld), or a PDA that also sent daily tailored feedback.

Two findings are worth knowing:

  • Electronic tracking helped people keep going. Adherence fell over time in every group, but more PDA users stayed in the high-adherence range than paper users. At 18 months, about 19 to 20% of PDA users were still logging at least 30% of the time, compared with 8% of the paper group.
  • The tool mattered less than the habit. At 24 months, weight change was not significantly different between the three groups. Across all groups, people who logged at least 60% of the time lost more weight than people who logged less than 30% of the time.

So the honest summary is: a digital tool may make it easier to keep logging, but the logging is what counts. The trial was about general weight-loss self-monitoring, not GLP-1 medicines, and a 2000s handheld is not a 2026 phone app. Still, the lesson travels well.

Where does a paper journal win?

Privacy you can see. A notebook does not have a privacy policy because it does not need one. Nobody processes it, syncs it or stores it on a server. If the idea of any company holding your weight and medication history makes you uneasy, paper removes the question entirely. (If you do use apps, our guide to what a GLP-1 app privacy policy should tell you shows what to look for.)

Total freedom of format. Want to track how your favorite jeans fit, rate your food noise with little drawings, or write half a page about a rough Thursday? Paper never says "this field only accepts numbers."

No screen at the moment you want a break from screens. Some people find that writing by hand at night is calming, while opening yet another app is not.

Nothing to learn and nothing to renew. No updates, no subscription screens, no password resets.

Where does an app win?

Remembering for you. Most weekly GLP-1 injections are taken on the same day each week, and a missed day is easy when your routine shifts. A reminder that shows up on your lock screen beats a notebook that is sitting in a drawer. Our step-by-step on how to track your GLP-1 shots covers what to log for each one.

Doing the math. Daily weight jumps around for many reasons. An app can show the trend line over weeks, so one salty dinner does not ruin your mood. On paper you would need a calculator and patience.

Rotating injection sites. Remembering whether last week was left abdomen or right thigh is surprisingly hard after a month. An app can show the last few sites at a glance.

Seeing patterns across things. Nausea the day after a shot? Lower appetite on high-protein days? When shots, symptoms, food and water live in one place, patterns are easier to spot. GLPily's AI helper, Pily, can answer questions from your own logs, which is hard to replicate with a notebook.

Not losing three months of notes. Notebooks get left in hotel rooms and soaked by water bottles. Data in an account survives a lost phone.

What does each one cost you in effort?

Paper costs effort later: adding up weeks, finding last month's symptom entry, copying numbers before an appointment. Apps cost effort earlier: downloading, setting up an account, granting notifications, and learning where things are.

If you have ever bought a beautiful planner in January and abandoned it in February, be honest with yourself about which kind of effort you are more likely to keep paying.

Can you use both?

Yes, and plenty of people do. A common hybrid:

  • App for the things that benefit from reminders and math: shot day, injection site, weight, water.
  • Notebook for the things that benefit from words: how you felt, what was hard, questions for your next visit.

The main risk with a hybrid is that your symptom notes end up split across two places. If you go hybrid, choose one home for side effects so you are not piecing it together in the waiting room. Our checklist of what to track on a GLP-1 can help you decide which items go where.

Which one should you pick?

Try this quick test:

  1. Have you missed a shot day in the last two months? Lean app.
  2. Would you feel uneasy with your health data in any company's account? Lean paper.
  3. Do you want charts to show your prescriber? Lean app.
  4. Do you journal for your mental health, not just for numbers? Keep a notebook either way.
  5. Do you hate typing on your phone? Lean paper.

Still undecided? Try the lightest option for two weeks and check in with yourself. If you want a three-way comparison that includes spreadsheets, see paper, spreadsheet or app.

Whatever you pick, neither a notebook nor an app replaces your prescriber. Questions about your dose, side effects that worry you, or anything urgent go to them or, in an emergency, to 911.

Common questions

Is a GLP-1 app more accurate than a paper journal?

Not by itself. Both are only as accurate as what you enter. An app can reduce some errors, such as wrong dates or math mistakes, and can remind you to log. Paper can be just as accurate if you write the date, time and units every time.

Do I need to pay for GLPily to replace a paper log?

No. Shot and pill logging, reminders, weight, symptoms, water and the journal are part of free tracking. PRO adds extras such as unlimited food photo scanning and the GLPily Grade.

What should I bring to appointments if I use paper?

The dates of your doses, any missed doses, your weights over time, and side effects with dates and how bad they were. Photographing the relevant pages before the visit gives you a backup.

Is paper safer for privacy?

In the sense that no company holds it, yes. But a notebook left on a kitchen counter can be read by anyone in the house, so store it where you would store anything personal.
