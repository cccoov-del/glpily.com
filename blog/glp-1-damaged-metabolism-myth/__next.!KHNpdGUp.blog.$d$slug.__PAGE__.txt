1c:T10da,<p>When weight loss slows to a crawl, or weight starts coming back, it can feel like the medicine &quot;broke&quot; your metabolism. It&#39;s an understandable fear, and it spreads fast online. But the research points to a less dramatic explanation: bodies naturally adjust to weight loss, and appetite changes when treatment changes. That&#39;s frustrating, but it isn&#39;t the same as damage.</p>
<h2 id="what-actually-changes-after-weight-loss">What actually changes after weight loss</h2>
<p>A smaller body generally needs less energy, both at rest and when moving, than a larger one. That&#39;s simple physics, not a malfunction. On top of that, the body tends to push back after weight loss: appetite can rise, and everyday movement can drop without you noticing.</p>
<p>There&#39;s also a smaller effect researchers call <strong>metabolic adaptation</strong>, where resting energy use falls a bit more than predicted from the weight lost. How big it is, and how much it matters, is debated. In one study of 171 women who lost about 12 kg in a weight-loss program, it averaged about 54 calories a day right after weight loss, was minimal once their weight had stabilized, and didn&#39;t predict who regained weight over two years. These adjustments help explain why keeping weight off is hard for many people, but they happen with any kind of weight loss and aren&#39;t unique to GLP-1 medicines.</p>
<h2 id="why-weight-often-returns-after-stopping">Why weight often returns after stopping</h2>
<p>This is where the &quot;damaged metabolism&quot; idea often comes from. In the STEP 1 extension, adults who stopped semaglutide 2.4 mg after 68 weeks regained about two-thirds of the weight they&#39;d lost within a year. Real-world data look similar: in a US health-system study of people prescribed GLP-1 medicines for obesity, those who stopped within two years ended up with weight loss similar to people who never started. That fits with the medicine&#39;s effects on appetite fading once it&#39;s stopped, which is the same pattern seen when treatment for many long-term conditions ends. It doesn&#39;t show that the medicine left the body worse off than before. We cover the details in <a href="/blog/what-happens-when-you-stop-semaglutide/">what happens when you stop semaglutide</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-about-slowing-down-while-still-on-treatment">What about slowing down while still on treatment?</h2>
<p>Weight loss on GLP-1 medicines typically slows and levels off over time. That&#39;s expected, not a sign that something broke (see <a href="/blog/glp-1-weight-loss-plateau/">why GLP-1 weight loss can plateau</a>). Response also varies a lot from person to person. A 2026 systematic review of low response to these medicines found links with factors like higher starting weight, blood sugar and insulin resistance, genetics, sex and age, and especially how consistently and how long people stayed on treatment. None of that supports a simple diagnosis of &quot;damaged metabolism.&quot;</p>
<h2 id="check-the-timeframe-before-the-conclusion">Check the timeframe before the conclusion</h2>
<p>Day-to-day weight jumps around with fluid, salt, bowel contents, menstrual cycles, sleep, stress and other medicines. Look at the trend across several weeks before deciding anything has changed. A sustained shift is worth bringing to your clinician, especially if it comes with new symptoms (like unusual tiredness, feeling cold, or hair or skin changes) or changes in your glucose control.</p>
<h2 id="what-can-actually-help">What can actually help</h2>
<p>A clinician can review your medicine, how long you&#39;ve been on it, missed doses, other prescriptions, sleep, health markers and whether your goals are realistic. If symptoms suggest something like thyroid disease or anemia, they can decide whether testing makes sense. A dietitian can help make sure you&#39;re eating enough protein and nutrients, and strength training may help preserve muscle as you lose weight.</p>
<p>What doesn&#39;t help: extreme restriction, stimulants, &quot;metabolism reset&quot; supplements, or taking more medicine than prescribed. Plateaus and regain can feel discouraging, but they aren&#39;t proof your body is broken. The better question is what&#39;s driving the trend, and which next step is safe for you.</p>
