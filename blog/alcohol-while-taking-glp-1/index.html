Alcohol While Taking a GLP-1: Questions About Nausea, Glucose and Safety

Can you drink alcohol on a GLP-1? What labels say, how alcohol can affect nausea, blood sugar and the pancreas, and what to ask your clinician.

"Can I still have a glass of wine?" is one of the most common GLP-1 questions, and a fair one. The US labels for Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro and Zepbound don't list alcohol as a drug interaction. That isn't the same as "no considerations," though. Alcohol can overlap with GLP-1 side effects, diabetes treatment and pancreas health in ways worth talking through with your clinician.

Alcohol and stomach side effects

Alcohol can irritate the stomach and may worsen nausea, reflux or dehydration for some people. If your GLP-1 medicine is already causing digestive symptoms or has sharply reduced your appetite, drinking may also make it harder to notice whether you're eating and drinking enough. These are practical considerations, not proof of a direct drug–alcohol interaction.

If you do drink, having something to eat and alternating drinks with water are common-sense habits. For everyday fluid tips, see hydration on GLP-1.

If you have diabetes, there's an extra layer

People taking insulin or sulfonylureas can already be at risk of low blood sugar, and GLP-1 medicines can add to that risk when combined with them. According to the American Diabetes Association, alcohol can also cause low blood sugar in people on these medicines, sometimes hours after the last drink. Many low-blood-sugar symptoms, like confusion and dizziness, can look like being drunk, which makes them easy to miss.

If you have diabetes, follow your care team's monitoring plan and ask whether any specific precautions apply to your medicines. Don't rely on how you feel alone to spot a low. Our guide to GLP-1 medicines and low blood sugar explains who is most at risk.

What about the pancreas and liver?

Heavy alcohol use is one of the most common causes of both acute and chronic pancreatitis, according to the NIDDK. GLP-1 medicine labels separately warn about acute pancreatitis. That doesn't prove the two combine in a specific way at every level of drinking, but it's a good reason to be open about your alcohol use. Heavy drinking can also harm the liver. If you've had pancreatitis, liver disease or a history of heavy drinking, discuss it before assuming anything about safety.

Make it an individual decision

Check the patient leaflet for your exact medicine, be honest with your clinician about how much and how often you drink, and ask how alcohol fits with your other conditions and prescriptions.

You may have seen headlines about GLP-1 medicines and alcohol cravings. That research is still early and these medicines aren't approved to treat alcohol use disorder (more in could GLP-1 medicines affect alcohol cravings?). If alcohol is causing harm or feels hard to control, effective support exists today, and you don't need to wait for new research. NIAAA's Alcohol Treatment Navigator is one place to start.

When to get urgent care

Seek urgent care for severe stomach pain that won't go away (especially if it spreads to your back), repeated vomiting, confusion, fainting or other serious symptoms. If you have diabetes and suspect low blood sugar, treat it as your care team has advised and get help if it doesn't improve.

A blog can't tell you whether alcohol is safe for you personally. The right answer depends on your medicine, your health history and your clinician's advice.
