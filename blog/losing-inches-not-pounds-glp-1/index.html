Losing Inches but Not Pounds on GLP-1: What Could Explain It?

Measurements can change differently from scale weight. Learn about fluid shifts, body composition, measurement limits and when to review progress.

♡By GLPily Editorial Team
Oct 11, 2026

A waistband can feel looser even when the number on the scale barely moves. That can happen for several reasons, including normal fluid shifts, changes in body composition, bowel contents, menstrual-cycle timing or differences in how measurements are taken. Different progress markers can move at different speeds, but a single measurement can't tell you exactly what changed.

The scale measures total body mass

A scale doesn't distinguish fat, muscle, fluid, food in your digestive tract or anything else. Day-to-day swings of a kilogram or more can come from water and digestion alone (here's more on why weight changes from day to day). Tape measurements have their own quirks: where you place the tape, posture, breathing and time of day all affect the result. If you track measurements, use the same spot, the same time of day and similar conditions each time, and don't read too much into differences of a centimeter or so.

Waist size is a real outcome in GLP-1 research, not just a home hack. In the SURMOUNT-5 trial in adults with obesity without diabetes, average waist circumference fell by about 18 cm with tirzepatide and 13 cm with semaglutide over 72 weeks, alongside average weight loss of about 20% and 14%. Those are group averages from a trial, not a timeline for any individual.

Why body composition matters during weight loss

Weight loss involves both fat mass and lean mass. A 2026 meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials found that lean mass made up roughly a quarter to a third of the weight lost with semaglutide, tirzepatide or liraglutide, a similar proportion to intensive lifestyle programs. Programs that added resistance training showed a lower share of lean-mass loss. That's one reason nutrition, protein and strength training come up in GLP-1 care. It doesn't mean everyone is losing too much muscle, and a home tape measure or bathroom scale can't diagnose muscle change.

What if measurements change but weight doesn't?

Give it time and check that you're measuring consistently before drawing conclusions. If you weigh yourself, look at the trend over several weeks rather than single days. Talk to your clinician about your goals, and mention it promptly if you also notice weakness, poor intake, signs of dehydration, new swelling or other symptoms. Don't cut food drastically or pile on intense exercise just to force the scale to move.

Make progress tracking serve you

Pick a small set of markers that are useful and feel manageable. For one person that might be a weight trend and staying on schedule with doses; for another, waist measurement, mobility, blood pressure or a diabetes marker agreed with a clinician. More data isn't always better if it adds stress.

Measurements are information, not a verdict. Different markers answer different questions, and none replaces an individual clinical assessment.
