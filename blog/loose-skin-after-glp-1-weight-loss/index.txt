18:Te21,<p>Loose skin can happen after substantial weight loss, whether the change comes from medication, surgery or other means. How skin responds depends on how much weight changes, age, genetics, how long the higher weight was carried, sun exposure, smoking and other factors. It is not a sign of failure, and not everyone experiences it to the same degree.</p>
<h2 id="is-it-caused-by-the-medicine">Is it caused by the medicine?</h2>
<p>So far, the evidence points to weight loss itself rather than a drug-specific effect. A 2026 review of GLP-1 medicines in aesthetic surgery concluded that the facial volume loss and skin laxity popularly called “Ozempic face” more plausibly reflect rapid or substantial weight loss than a proven effect of the medicine on the skin. In other words, similar changes can follow any large weight loss.</p>
<h2 id="what-can-and-cannot-change-skin">What can and cannot change skin</h2>
<p>Strength training can help preserve or build muscle when it&#39;s appropriate for you, which may change the shape beneath the skin (see <a href="/blog/glp-1-strength-training-muscle/">why strength training can matter on GLP-1</a>). It can&#39;t reliably remove significant excess skin. Moisturizers can help with dryness and comfort, but no over-the-counter cream has been shown to eliminate substantial loose skin. Skin may continue to adapt for a while, but how much varies a lot.</p>
<h2 id="avoid-miracle-claims">Avoid miracle claims</h2>
<p>Be skeptical of supplements, wraps, detoxes or creams that promise to “tighten skin in days.” These claims often rely on before-and-after photos, lighting or posture rather than independent evidence (we explain why in <a href="/blog/before-after-glp-1-stories-limitations/">what before-and-after stories leave out</a>).</p>
<h2 id="when-loose-skin-causes-physical-problems">When loose skin causes physical problems</h2>
<p>Excess skin can trap moisture and lead to rashes, irritation or infections in skin folds, and it can sometimes make hygiene, exercise or movement uncomfortable. Keeping folds clean and dry helps, and a clinician can treat skin infections or irritation. If problems keep coming back, ask about a dermatology or plastic-surgery assessment.</p>
<h2 id="body-image-is-part-of-the-experience">Body image is part of the experience</h2>
<p>You can feel pleased about health changes and still have mixed feelings about a body that looks different. You don&#39;t have to feel grateful for every change or rush into a cosmetic decision. Support from a clinician, counselor or trusted person can help if body-image concerns become distressing.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-a-professional">What to ask a professional</h2>
<p>Surgery to remove excess skin, such as abdominoplasty (a tummy tuck) or other body-contouring procedures, is major surgery with risks including poor healing, scarring and fluid build-up. Surgeons generally want weight to be stable first. If you&#39;re considering it, ask about timing, likely benefits and limits, recovery, risks, cost and whether insurance may cover it when there&#39;s a medical reason. Tell the surgical team you take a GLP-1 medicine, because these medicines slow stomach emptying and the anesthesia team needs to plan for that; follow their instructions rather than stopping or changing a dose on your own. Avoid choosing a provider based only on social media or financing offers.</p>
<p>Loose skin is a common topic in weight-loss stories, but every body responds differently. Realistic options can help, without the message that you need to “fix” your body to validate your progress.</p>
