15:Tef9,<p>Some people feel almost nothing after starting a GLP-1 medicine and start to worry: if I&#39;m not nauseous, is it even working? Others see nausea as a sign the medicine is &quot;doing its job.&quot; It&#39;s an easy assumption to make, but the trial data don&#39;t back it up.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-trials-found">What the trials found</h2>
<p>Two large analyses looked directly at this question:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Semaglutide 2.4 mg (Wegovy):</strong> pooling the STEP 1–3 trials (68 weeks, adults with overweight or obesity), average weight loss on semaglutide was similar in people who had digestive side effects (11.4%–17.7%) and those who didn&#39;t (9.6%–17.1%). A statistical analysis estimated that side effects accounted for less than 1 percentage point of the extra weight lost compared with placebo.</li>
<li><strong>Tirzepatide (Zepbound):</strong> across the SURMOUNT-1 to -4 trials, weight loss was similar whether people reported no nausea, nausea alone, or any nausea, vomiting or diarrhea. Those symptoms were estimated to account for only a small share (up to about 3%) of the total weight reduction.</li>
</ul>
<p>Both were secondary analyses of trial data, written with the manufacturers involved, and they describe group averages, not guarantees for any one person. Still, they point the same way: for most people, weight loss on these medicines isn&#39;t driven by feeling sick.</p>
<h2 id="why-side-effects-vary-so-much">Why side effects vary so much</h2>
<p>Digestive side effects depend on the specific medicine, dose, how far into treatment you are, other health conditions and plain individual variation. In those same semaglutide trials, nausea was reported by 43.9% of people on the medicine, which also means more than half didn&#39;t report it. Having few or no side effects is a common experience, not a red flag.</p>
<h2 id="so-how-do-you-know-it-39-s-working">So how do you know it&#39;s working?</h2>
<p>It depends on why you&#39;re taking it. Depending on the medicine and your goals, your clinician might look at your weight trend over months, blood sugar measures such as HbA1c, blood pressure, or other health goals you&#39;ve agreed on. One weigh-in, or one good or bad week of symptoms, can&#39;t stand in for that bigger picture. Our guide to <a href="/blog/what-counts-as-response-weight-loss-medication/">what counts as a response to weight-loss medication</a> goes into how clinicians think about it.</p>
<p>Missed doses, interruptions in supply and how long you&#39;ve been on treatment can also shape results, so those are worth tracking too.</p>
<h2 id="please-don-39-t-chase-nausea">Please don&#39;t chase nausea</h2>
<p>Never increase a dose, shorten the time between doses, or add another medicine to try to &quot;feel it working.&quot; More nausea isn&#39;t a goal, and taking more than prescribed can be dangerous. The same goes the other way: don&#39;t stop a prescribed medicine just because you feel fine, unless your clinician recommends a change.</p>
<h2 id="and-if-you-do-have-side-effects-but-little-progress">And if you do have side effects but little progress?</h2>
<p>That&#39;s a different, and equally valid, frustration. We cover it in <a href="/blog/side-effects-no-weight-loss-glp-1/">side effects but no weight loss on GLP-1</a>.</p>
<h2 id="questions-to-bring-to-your-next-appointment">Questions to bring to your next appointment</h2>
<ul>
<li>How long is this stage of treatment expected to last?</li>
<li>Which results will we use to judge whether it&#39;s helping, and when?</li>
<li>What should prompt me to get in touch before then?</li>
</ul>
<p>The simplest answer is the right one: side effects aren&#39;t a scorecard. The goal is a meaningful benefit with side effects you can live with, and for many people that includes having very few.</p>
