15:T1148,<p><strong>Can a GLP-1 tracking app replace your doctor?</strong> No. A tracker is very good at remembering what happened: dose dates, side effects, weight trends. Deciding what it <em>means</em> and what to do about it, such as changing a dose, pausing a medicine or investigating a symptom, belongs to your prescriber, who knows your full history and can examine you.</p>
<p>That is not a modest disclaimer we are obliged to write. It is genuinely the most useful way to think about a tracker, including ours.</p>
<h2 id="what-is-a-tracking-app-actually-good-at">What is a tracking app actually good at?</h2>
<p>Human memory is a terrible medical record. Ask anyone to recall which day last month they felt nauseated and you will get a confident, wrong answer. Apps fix that. They are good at:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Remembering.</strong> Exact dates and times of doses, missed doses, injection sites.</li>
<li><strong>Reminding.</strong> Shot day, water, a daily check-in.</li>
<li><strong>Summarizing.</strong> Turning sixty weigh-ins into a trend line.</li>
<li><strong>Spotting simple patterns.</strong> &quot;Your nausea entries cluster on the day after your shot.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Preparing.</strong> Putting your questions and notes in one place before an appointment.</li>
</ul>
<p>All of that makes the conversation with your clinician faster and more accurate. Our guide to <a href="/blog/share-glp-1-tracking-report-with-clinician/">sharing a tracking report with your clinician</a> covers what to bring.</p>

<p>Notice the pattern: the app answers &quot;what happened,&quot; the prescriber answers &quot;what should happen.&quot; For missed doses specifically, our post on what each label says about a missed GLP-1 dose summarizes the official instructions, but your prescriber or pharmacist should confirm them for you.</p>
<h2 id="why-can-39-t-an-app-make-those-calls">Why can&#39;t an app make those calls?</h2>
<p>A few reasons, and none of them are about apps being dumb:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>An app only sees what you type.</strong> It does not know your blood tests, your kidney function, other diagnoses, or the medicine your cardiologist started last week, unless you entered all of it perfectly.</li>
<li><strong>Symptoms have more than one cause.</strong> Stomach pain the day after a shot might be a common side effect, or it might be something that needs urgent attention. Telling them apart can need questions, an exam or tests.</li>
<li><strong>Some numbers in apps are estimates.</strong> For example, &quot;medication level&quot; charts are models based on typical drug behavior, not measurements from your body. Our post on <a href="/blog/glp-1-estimated-medication-level-charts/">estimated medication-level charts</a> explains what they can and cannot tell you.</li>
<li><strong>Regulators draw a line here too.</strong> The FDA focuses its oversight on medical software whose functions could pose a risk to patient safety, such as software that diagnoses conditions. For software that helps people self-manage a condition <em>without providing specific treatment suggestions</em>, the FDA generally does not intend to enforce device requirements. A tracker that started telling you what dose to take would be on the other side of that line.</li>
</ul>

1c:T116c,<h2 id="what-about-ai-helpers-in-health-apps">What about AI helpers in health apps?</h2>
<p>AI chat makes the line blurrier, because it sounds so sure of itself. A sensible AI helper in a tracking app should:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Answer from your logs:</strong> &quot;You logged nausea on 4 of the last 6 shot days.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Quote official facts:</strong> what the label says about schedules, storage or missed doses, with a nudge to confirm with your prescriber.</li>
<li><strong>Decline personal dose decisions:</strong> &quot;Should I double up?&quot; or &quot;Should I skip this week?&quot; should get &quot;That&#39;s one for your prescriber or pharmacist,&quot; not a yes or no.</li>
<li><strong>Point to help for red flags</strong> instead of reassuring you.</li>
</ul>
<p>That is the line we use for Pily, GLPily&#39;s AI helper, which answers questions from your own logs. It can tell you what you logged and what the label says; it does not make personal dosing decisions, and it gives general information, not medical advice.</p>
<h2 id="when-should-you-skip-the-app-and-call-someone">When should you skip the app and call someone?</h2>
<p>Logging is for things that can wait until your next appointment. Some things cannot:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A medical emergency</strong> (trouble breathing, collapse, chest pain, signs of a severe allergic reaction): call <strong>911</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>You think you took too much or the wrong medicine:</strong> call <strong>Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222</strong> (free and confidential) or your prescriber. Our guide on <a href="/blog/wrong-dose-glp-1-what-to-do/">what to do if you think you took the wrong amount</a> has the next steps.</li>
<li><strong>Severe or persistent symptoms</strong>, such as severe stomach pain or vomiting you cannot keep fluids down: contact your prescriber promptly or seek urgent care. Our posts on <a href="/blog/vomiting-on-glp-1-warning-signs/">vomiting warning signs</a> and <a href="/blog/pancreatitis-glp-1-medicines/">pancreatitis</a> explain what the labels flag.</li>
</ul>
<p>A quick rule: if you are wondering whether to log it or call about it, call. You can always log it afterwards.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-you-make-the-app-and-the-appointment-work-together">How do you make the app and the appointment work together?</h2>
<p>A simple routine that respects the boundary:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Log daily, briefly.</strong> Doses, symptoms with a 1–5 severity, weight if you track it.</li>
<li><strong>Write questions as they come up.</strong> In the app&#39;s journal or a notes field, the moment you think of them.</li>
<li><strong>Before the appointment, review the last few weeks.</strong> What changed? What do you want to decide together? Our three-month check-in guide has a good list.</li>
<li><strong>Bring the summary, not the scroll.</strong> One page beats forty screenshots.</li>
<li><strong>After the appointment, update the app</strong> with any changes your prescriber made: new dose, new instructions, new reminders.</li>
</ol>
<p>Our side-effect tracking guide shows how to log symptoms so your clinician gets the full picture.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="can-an-ai-chatbot-tell-me-whether-to-increase-my-glp-1-dose">Can an AI chatbot tell me whether to increase my GLP-1 dose?</h3>
<p>It should not. Dose changes depend on your response, side effects, other conditions and your prescriber&#39;s plan. An AI can help you prepare the question; your prescriber answers it.</p>
<h3 id="are-tracking-apps-regulated-by-the-fda">Are tracking apps regulated by the FDA?</h3>
<p>It depends on what the software does. The FDA focuses on software with medical functions that could pose a risk to patients, like diagnosing conditions. Apps that help you log and self-manage without giving specific treatment suggestions are generally not its focus.</p>
<h3 id="is-it-still-worth-tracking-if-my-clinician-never-looks-at-the-app">Is it still worth tracking if my clinician never looks at the app?</h3>
<p>Yes. Tracking helps <em>you</em> remember and describe what happened, which makes any conversation better, even if you never show the screen.</p>
<h3 id="what-if-my-app-and-my-clinician-disagree">What if my app and my clinician disagree?</h3>
<p>Go with your clinician, and tell them what the app showed. If it seems important, ask how they interpret it. They may have context the app cannot see.</p>
