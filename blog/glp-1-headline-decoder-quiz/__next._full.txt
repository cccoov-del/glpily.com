17:T22c1,<p>This GLP-1 headline quiz gives you 10 news-style claims about Wegovy, Ozempic, Zepbound and friends, and asks one question: is it <strong>fact</strong>, <strong>spin</strong>, or <strong>missing context</strong>? Each answer shows the real numbers from the trial, label or regulator, so you finish with sharper instincts, not just a score.</p>
<p>The headlines are invented in the style of real ones. The facts in the answers are not. Grab a pen, keep score, and don&#39;t peek.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-play-the-glp-1-headline-decoder">How to play the GLP-1 headline decoder</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fact:</strong> the headline is accurate and fair.</li>
<li><strong>Spin:</strong> the headline bends a real finding into something bigger, scarier or rosier than it is.</li>
<li><strong>Missing context:</strong> the headline is technically true, but something you need to judge it was left out (a baseline, a population, a date).</li>
</ul>
<p>Some headlines fit more than one. Pick the <em>best</em> verdict. One point each.</p>
<h3 id="1-weight-loss-shot-cuts-heart-attacks-and-strokes-by-20">1. &quot;Weight-loss shot cuts heart attacks and strokes by 20%&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>C) Missing context.</strong> The 20% is real. In the SELECT trial, 17,604 adults with existing heart disease and overweight or obesity, but no diabetes, took semaglutide 2.4 mg or placebo for about 40 months. A heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death happened to <strong>6.5%</strong> of the semaglutide group and <strong>8.0%</strong> of the placebo group. That&#39;s a 20% <em>relative</em> drop and about 1.5 percentage points in <em>absolute</em> terms. Both numbers matter, and the result applies to people like those studied. Our <a href="/blog/relative-vs-absolute-risk-glp-1/">relative vs absolute risk explainer</a> walks through the math.</p>
</details>

<h3 id="2-diabetes-drug-doubles-your-risk-of-sudden-blindness">2. &quot;Diabetes drug DOUBLES your risk of sudden blindness&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>B) Spin.</strong> In June 2025 the European Medicines Agency concluded that NAION, an eye condition that can cause sudden vision loss, is a <em>very rare</em> side effect of semaglutide. Observational studies in adults with type 2 diabetes suggested about double the risk. In absolute terms, EMA put that at roughly <strong>one additional case per 10,000 person-years</strong> of treatment. &quot;Doubles&quot; is accurate. &quot;Your risk of sudden blindness&quot; turns a very rare event into an everyday one. The fact worth keeping: if your vision suddenly drops or gets worse fast, contact a doctor promptly.</p>
</details>

<h3 id="3-fda-weight-loss-drugs-linked-to-suicidal-thoughts">3. &quot;FDA: Weight-loss drugs linked to suicidal thoughts&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>C) Missing context, and it&#39;s out of date.</strong> On January 13, 2026, FDA asked the makers of Saxenda, Wegovy and Zepbound to <em>remove</em> the suicidal behavior and ideation warning from their labels. FDA&#39;s meta-analysis of 91 placebo-controlled trials with 107,910 participants found no increased risk, and a large Sentinel study of more than 2.2 million new users found no increased risk of intentional self-harm. A headline like this one may be recycling a 2023 story. FDA still says anyone with thoughts of self-harm should be referred for mental health care. In the US, call or text 988 any time.</p>
</details>

<h3 id="4-hundreds-of-reports-link-glp-1-drugs-to-scary-condition">4. &quot;Hundreds of reports link GLP-1 drugs to [scary condition]&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>C) Missing context.</strong> Reports to FDA&#39;s adverse event system come from patients, clinicians and companies, and FDA itself says there is &quot;no certainty&quot; that a suspected drug caused the event. The event could come from the illness, another medicine or chance. Reports can flag a signal worth studying, but they aren&#39;t proof. See <a href="/blog/adverse-event-report-causation-glp-1/">why an adverse-event report is not proof</a>.</p>
</details>

<h3 id="5-study-wegovy-users-lose-15-of-their-body-weight">5. &quot;Study: Wegovy users lose 15% of their body weight&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>B) Spin.</strong> In the 68-week trial on the Wegovy label, <em>average</em> weight change was <strong>−14.9%</strong> with semaglutide and <strong>−2.4%</strong> with placebo. Averages hide a spread: <strong>47.9%</strong> of people on the medicine lost 15% or more, <strong>66.1%</strong> lost at least 10%, and <strong>83.5%</strong> lost at least 5%. &quot;Users lose 15%&quot; makes an average sound like a promise. More on this in <a href="/blog/average-weight-loss-vs-responder-rates/">average weight loss vs responder rates</a>.</p>
</details>

<h3 id="6-stop-the-shot-and-the-weight-comes-back">6. &quot;Stop the shot and the weight comes back&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>A) Fact, mostly.</strong> In the STEP 1 extension, people who stopped semaglutide 2.4 mg had regained about <strong>two-thirds</strong> of their lost weight a year later. They were still about 5.6% below their starting weight on average. Small print: the extension followed 327 people and was exploratory. A fair headline says &quot;much of it, on average,&quot; not &quot;all of it, for everyone.&quot;</p>
</details>

<h3 id="7-ozempic-approved-for-weight-loss">7. &quot;Ozempic approved for weight loss&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>B) Spin (or just wrong).</strong> Ozempic&#39;s label covers adults with <strong>type 2 diabetes</strong>: blood sugar control plus heart and kidney risk reduction in that group. The semaglutide brand approved for weight management is <strong>Wegovy</strong>. People often use &quot;Ozempic&quot; as slang for the whole class, but headlines shouldn&#39;t.</p>
</details>

<h3 id="8-first-glp-1-pill-for-weight-loss-approved">8. &quot;First GLP-1 pill for weight loss approved&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>A) Fact, if it&#39;s dated December 2025.</strong> Novo Nordisk announced FDA approval of Wegovy tablets on December 22, 2025. Then on April 1, 2026, FDA approved Lilly&#39;s Foundayo (orforglipron). So &quot;first&quot; was true once and is now history. The two pills come with different instructions: Wegovy tablets are taken on an empty stomach with no more than 4 ounces of water, followed by a 30-minute wait. Foundayo can be taken with or without food. Our <a href="/blog/new-weight-loss-medicines-2026/">2026 approvals roundup</a> has the full list.</p>
</details>

<h3 id="9-weight-loss-shots-cause-thyroid-cancer">9. &quot;Weight-loss shots cause thyroid cancer&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>C) Missing context.</strong> These labels carry a boxed warning because the drugs caused thyroid C-cell tumors <strong>in rodents</strong>. The labels say it&#39;s <strong>unknown</strong> whether that happens in humans. They also say people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2 shouldn&#39;t use them. That&#39;s a real precaution, not a proven cause.</p>
</details>

<h3 id="10-next-gen-retatrutide-now-available-online">10. &quot;Next-gen retatrutide now available online&quot;</h3>
<p>A) Fact  B) Spin  C) Missing context</p>
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

<p><strong>B) Spin, and a warning sign.</strong> Retatrutide is still <strong>investigational</strong>, which means it isn&#39;t FDA-approved for any use. FDA has warned companies selling retatrutide and similar products labeled &quot;for research purposes&quot; or &quot;not for human consumption.&quot; The agency says the quality of those products is unknown and consumers shouldn&#39;t buy them. &quot;Available online&quot; here means &quot;sold outside the system.&quot;</p>
</details>

<strong>3–5:</strong> Normal human. Headlines are written to be clicked, not to be fair.

1f:T829,<h2 id="the-three-questions-that-decode-almost-any-glp-1-headline">The three questions that decode almost any GLP-1 headline</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Out of how many?</strong> A percentage without a baseline is half a number.</li>
<li><strong>Who was studied, and for how long?</strong> SELECT studied people with heart disease and without diabetes. The EMA eye estimate came from people with type 2 diabetes.</li>
<li><strong>When was this true?</strong> GLP-1 news moves fast. 2026 alone brought a label-warning removal, new pills and higher doses.</li>
</ol>
<p>If you track your own treatment, keep your notes as careful as these answers: date, dose and what you noticed. That way your next appointment runs on your data, not on a headline. A log like the one in GLPily can hold that alongside weight and symptoms.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="why-do-glp-1-headlines-use-relative-risk-so-often">Why do GLP-1 headlines use relative risk so often?</h3>
<p>Relative numbers are usually bigger and more dramatic, so they make punchier headlines. They aren&#39;t wrong, but you need the absolute numbers next to them. In SELECT, &quot;20% lower&quot; and &quot;6.5% vs 8.0%&quot; describe the same result.</p>
<h3 id="where-can-i-check-a-glp-1-headline-myself">Where can I check a GLP-1 headline myself?</h3>
<p>Start with the primary source: the FDA label on DailyMed, FDA drug safety communications, the EMA news page, or the original journal article. If a story doesn&#39;t link to any of these, treat it as unverified until you find one.</p>
<h3 id="does-a-very-rare-side-effect-mean-i-can-ignore-it">Does a &quot;very rare&quot; side effect mean I can ignore it?</h3>
<p>No. It means it&#39;s unlikely, not impossible. Learn the warning signs listed on your label, like sudden vision changes, and ask your prescriber or pharmacist what applies to you.</p>
<h3 id="is-this-quiz-medical-advice">Is this quiz medical advice?</h3>
<p>No. It&#39;s a media-literacy game. Decisions about starting, stopping or changing a medicine belong with your prescriber.</p>
