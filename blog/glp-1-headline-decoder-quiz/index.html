GLP-1 Headline Quiz: Fact, Spin or Missing Context?

Ten news-style GLP-1 headlines, three possible verdicts and the real numbers behind each one. Can you tell a fact from a spin from a headline that left out the important part?

Oct 27, 2026

This GLP-1 headline quiz gives you 10 news-style claims about Wegovy, Ozempic, Zepbound and friends, and asks one question: is it fact, spin, or missing context? Each answer shows the real numbers from the trial, label or regulator, so you finish with sharper instincts, not just a score.

The headlines are invented in the style of real ones. The facts in the answers are not. Grab a pen, keep score, and don't peek.

How to play the GLP-1 headline decoder

  • Fact: the headline is accurate and fair.
  • Spin: the headline bends a real finding into something bigger, scarier or rosier than it is.
  • Missing context: the headline is technically true, but something you need to judge it was left out (a baseline, a population, a date).

Some headlines fit more than one. Pick the best verdict. One point each.

1. "Weight-loss shot cuts heart attacks and strokes by 20%"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

C) Missing context. The 20% is real. In the SELECT trial, 17,604 adults with existing heart disease and overweight or obesity, but no diabetes, took semaglutide 2.4 mg or placebo for about 40 months. A heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death happened to 6.5% of the semaglutide group and 8.0% of the placebo group. That's a 20% relative drop and about 1.5 percentage points in absolute terms. Both numbers matter, and the result applies to people like those studied. Our relative vs absolute risk explainer walks through the math.

2. "Diabetes drug DOUBLES your risk of sudden blindness"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

B) Spin. In June 2025 the European Medicines Agency concluded that NAION, an eye condition that can cause sudden vision loss, is a very rare side effect of semaglutide. Observational studies in adults with type 2 diabetes suggested about double the risk. In absolute terms, EMA put that at roughly one additional case per 10,000 person-years of treatment. "Doubles" is accurate. "Your risk of sudden blindness" turns a very rare event into an everyday one. The fact worth keeping: if your vision suddenly drops or gets worse fast, contact a doctor promptly.

3. "FDA: Weight-loss drugs linked to suicidal thoughts"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

C) Missing context, and it's out of date. On January 13, 2026, FDA asked the makers of Saxenda, Wegovy and Zepbound to remove the suicidal behavior and ideation warning from their labels. FDA's meta-analysis of 91 placebo-controlled trials with 107,910 participants found no increased risk, and a large Sentinel study of more than 2.2 million new users found no increased risk of intentional self-harm. A headline like this one may be recycling a 2023 story. FDA still says anyone with thoughts of self-harm should be referred for mental health care. In the US, call or text 988 any time.

4. "Hundreds of reports link GLP-1 drugs to [scary condition]"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

C) Missing context. Reports to FDA's adverse event system come from patients, clinicians and companies, and FDA itself says there is "no certainty" that a suspected drug caused the event. The event could come from the illness, another medicine or chance. Reports can flag a signal worth studying, but they aren't proof. See why an adverse-event report is not proof.

5. "Study: Wegovy users lose 15% of their body weight"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

B) Spin. In the 68-week trial on the Wegovy label, average weight change was −14.9% with semaglutide and −2.4% with placebo. Averages hide a spread: 47.9% of people on the medicine lost 15% or more, 66.1% lost at least 10%, and 83.5% lost at least 5%. "Users lose 15%" makes an average sound like a promise. More on this in average weight loss vs responder rates.

6. "Stop the shot and the weight comes back"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

A) Fact, mostly. In the STEP 1 extension, people who stopped semaglutide 2.4 mg had regained about two-thirds of their lost weight a year later. They were still about 5.6% below their starting weight on average. Small print: the extension followed 327 people and was exploratory. A fair headline says "much of it, on average," not "all of it, for everyone."

7. "Ozempic approved for weight loss"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

B) Spin (or just wrong). Ozempic's label covers adults with type 2 diabetes: blood sugar control plus heart and kidney risk reduction in that group. The semaglutide brand approved for weight management is Wegovy. People often use "Ozempic" as slang for the whole class, but headlines shouldn't.

8. "First GLP-1 pill for weight loss approved"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

A) Fact, if it's dated December 2025. Novo Nordisk announced FDA approval of Wegovy tablets on December 22, 2025. Then on April 1, 2026, FDA approved Lilly's Foundayo (orforglipron). So "first" was true once and is now history. The two pills come with different instructions: Wegovy tablets are taken on an empty stomach with no more than 4 ounces of water, followed by a 30-minute wait. Foundayo can be taken with or without food. Our 2026 approvals roundup has the full list.

9. "Weight-loss shots cause thyroid cancer"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

C) Missing context. These labels carry a boxed warning because the drugs caused thyroid C-cell tumors in rodents. The labels say it's unknown whether that happens in humans. They also say people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2 shouldn't use them. That's a real precaution, not a proven cause.

10. "Next-gen retatrutide now available online"

A) Fact B) Spin C) Missing context

Show answer

B) Spin, and a warning sign. Retatrutide is still investigational, which means it isn't FDA-approved for any use. FDA has warned companies selling retatrutide and similar products labeled "for research purposes" or "not for human consumption." The agency says the quality of those products is unknown and consumers shouldn't buy them. "Available online" here means "sold outside the system."

Your score

9–10: Professional headline skeptic. Your group chat is lucky to have you. 6–8: Sharp eye. You catch most of the spin and some of the missing context. 3–5: Normal human. Headlines are written to be clicked, not to be fair. 0–2: Fresh start. Bookmark how to read a study headline in five minutes.

The three questions that decode almost any GLP-1 headline

  1. Out of how many? A percentage without a baseline is half a number.
  2. Who was studied, and for how long? SELECT studied people with heart disease and without diabetes. The EMA eye estimate came from people with type 2 diabetes.
  3. When was this true? GLP-1 news moves fast. 2026 alone brought a label-warning removal, new pills and higher doses.

If you track your own treatment, keep your notes as careful as these answers: date, dose and what you noticed. That way your next appointment runs on your data, not on a headline. A log like the one in GLPily can hold that alongside weight and symptoms.

Common questions

Why do GLP-1 headlines use relative risk so often?

Relative numbers are usually bigger and more dramatic, so they make punchier headlines. They aren't wrong, but you need the absolute numbers next to them. In SELECT, "20% lower" and "6.5% vs 8.0%" describe the same result.

Where can I check a GLP-1 headline myself?

Start with the primary source: the FDA label on DailyMed, FDA drug safety communications, the EMA news page, or the original journal article. If a story doesn't link to any of these, treat it as unverified until you find one.

Does a "very rare" side effect mean I can ignore it?

No. It means it's unlikely, not impossible. Learn the warning signs listed on your label, like sudden vision changes, and ask your prescriber or pharmacist what applies to you.

Is this quiz medical advice?

No. It's a media-literacy game. Decisions about starting, stopping or changing a medicine belong with your prescriber.
