GLP-1 Weight-Loss Plateau: Why Progress Can Slow

Weight loss on a GLP-1 often slows after a strong start. Why plateaus happen, how to tell one from normal noise and what to bring to a review.

♡By GLPily Editorial Team
Oct 11, 2026

Plateaus are normal ♡

On this page

  1. Why the curve flattens
  2. How to tell a plateau from noise
  3. What the evidence can and cannot tell you
  4. What to bring to a treatment review

A weight-loss plateau can feel like the floor has dropped out after weeks or months of progress. But weight change is rarely a straight line. As your body gets smaller, it generally needs less energy, and your daily habits may shift too—while fluid swings and normal day-to-day variation can hide a slower underlying trend. A plateau doesn't automatically mean the medicine has stopped working.

Why the curve flattens

Slowing down over time is common. For context, in the STEP 1 trial of semaglutide 2.4 mg (adults with overweight or obesity, without diabetes), average weight loss was about 15% at week 68.

Some of the earliest loss can also include water and stored carbohydrate, so later progress naturally looks slower. Appetite, activity, sleep, stress, other medicines, health conditions and access to food can change over time as well. Eventually many people reach a new balance point where intake and energy needs are closer together. That's physiology, not a sign of a lack of willpower.

How to tell a plateau from noise

A handful of weigh-ins can be misleading. If weighing is part of your plan, look at a consistent trend over several weeks, allowing for things like your menstrual cycle, travel, salty meals or constipation. Our explainer on day-to-day weight changes goes into this, and a weight trend calculator can smooth out the bumps.

Try not to respond to a few higher numbers with extreme restriction or punishing exercise. Other measures your clinician agrees are useful—waist size, glucose markers, blood pressure, fitness, how you feel—can tell a fuller story. Tracking progress beyond the scale can help here.

What the evidence can and cannot tell you

Trials report group averages under structured conditions. For example, in the SURMOUNT-5 trial of adults with obesity but without type 2 diabetes, average weight loss at 72 weeks was 20.2% with tirzepatide and 13.7% with semaglutide—and individuals within each group varied widely. Real-world results are often lower and more variable: in one US health-system study, people who stayed on a GLP-1 for two years lost about 9% of their weight on average, with large differences between people. Side effects, costs, treatment breaks and follow-up all play a part.

Research doesn't point to one universal moment when plateaus happen, or one trick that reliably restarts weight loss. A plateau deserves a review in context, not a one-size-fits-all fix.

What to bring to a treatment review

Share how long the plateau has lasted, your treatment timeline, any missed doses or supply gaps, side effects, your current goals, and changes in your health or other medicines. Ask how your response is being judged and whether the plan still fits. Please don't increase a dose or combine medicines on your own. If the medicine has become hard to tolerate, that belongs in the conversation too.

Slowing progress is common enough to plan for—it's not a personal failure. A good review asks what's changed and what matters most for your health next, not just how to force the scale down.
