Can GLP-1 Medicines Affect Other Oral Medications?

GLP-1 medicines slow stomach emptying, which can affect some oral medicines, including birth control pills with tirzepatide. Here is what US labels say.

GLP-1 medicines slow down how quickly the stomach empties. That's part of how they help people feel full, but it also raises a fair question: if food sits in the stomach longer, what about the other pills you take? The short answer is that most people won't need changes, a few specific situations do deserve attention, and the details differ by product.

What the US labels actually say

All the major US labels mention that slowed stomach emptying has the potential to affect how other oral medicines are absorbed. Beyond that, the wording differs:

  • Ozempic (semaglutide injection): in clinical pharmacology studies, semaglutide did not affect the absorption of oral medicines to any clinically relevant degree. The label still advises caution.
  • Wegovy (semaglutide): asks clinicians to monitor the effects of oral medicines and consider extra clinical or lab monitoring for drugs with a narrow therapeutic index (where small changes in blood levels matter) or that need monitoring anyway. It also notes that in a study with the semaglutide tablet, levothyroxine (thyroid hormone) exposure rose by 33%.
  • Mounjaro and Zepbound (tirzepatide): ask clinicians to monitor people taking oral medicines that depend on reaching a threshold level, or that have a narrow therapeutic index, with warfarin given as an example.

So "it may affect absorption" isn't the same as "every tablet stops working." It's a prompt to check the medicines where timing and blood levels really matter.

Birth control pills and tirzepatide

This one is specific and worth knowing. The US Mounjaro and Zepbound labels advise people using oral hormonal contraceptives to switch to a non-oral method, or add a barrier method such as condoms, for 4 weeks after starting tirzepatide and for 4 weeks after each dose increase. Non-oral hormonal methods (such as an implant, IUD, patch or ring) shouldn't be affected, according to the labels. Ask your prescriber or pharmacist what applies to you.

Oral semaglutide has its own rules

If your GLP-1 is itself a tablet, such as Wegovy tablets, the instructions are different again. The Wegovy label says to take the tablet on an empty stomach with a small amount of water and wait at least 30 minutes before eating, drinking anything else, or taking other oral medicines. Our comparison of GLP-1 pills and injections explains why absorption works differently.

Diabetes medicines deserve a closer look

This isn't about stomach emptying, but it's one of the most important combinations to review. GLP-1 medicines can raise the risk of low blood sugar when used with insulin or sulfonylureas, and labels suggest clinicians consider lowering those doses when starting. Reduced appetite, vomiting or diarrhea can also change your usual routine. Follow your diabetes team's monitoring plan, and see our guide to GLP-1 medicines and low blood sugar.

Before surgery or sedation

Slowed stomach emptying also matters before procedures. Labels mention rare reports of pulmonary aspiration (stomach contents entering the lungs) in people on GLP-1 medicines having general anesthesia or deep sedation, even when they had followed fasting instructions. Always tell the surgical or procedure team that you take a GLP-1 medicine, well before the day.

What to do (and not do)

Give your prescriber and pharmacist a complete list: prescriptions, over-the-counter products and supplements, including how and when you take them. Ask specifically whether any of them need timing changes or extra monitoring.

Please don't move doses, crush tablets, change food timing or stop a prescription based on a general article, including this one. Keep following each medicine's own instructions until you get individual advice.

When to get in touch

Contact your care team promptly if a treated condition suddenly seems poorly controlled (for example, seizures, irregular heart rhythm or unusual bleeding on a blood thinner), or if you have repeated vomiting, can't keep fluids down or have severe stomach pain. Emergency symptoms need urgent care. An interaction isn't the only possible explanation, which is why a clinician should assess it.

The goal isn't to fear every pill, but to check the combinations that matter. A pharmacist is often the fastest way to find out whether yours do.
