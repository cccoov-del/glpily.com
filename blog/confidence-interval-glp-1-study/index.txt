17:T11a3,<p>A confidence interval (CI) is the range in parentheses after a study result, such as &quot;-12.4 percentage points (95% CI, -13.4 to -11.5)&quot;. It shows how precisely the study pinned down the average effect: a narrow range means a precise estimate, a wide range means more uncertainty, and for ratios, a range that includes 1.0 means the data are compatible with no difference at all.</p>
<p>That is the whole idea. You do not need to calculate anything; you just need to read the parentheses. Let&#39;s practice on real GLP-1 trials.</p>
<h2 id="what-does-a-95-confidence-interval-actually-mean">What does a 95% confidence interval actually mean?</h2>
<p>A trial measures a sample of people, not everyone on Earth. If you could rerun the same trial many times, you would get slightly different answers each time. A 95% CI is built so that, across many such repeats, 95% of the intervals calculated this way would contain the true average effect.</p>
<p>In plain English: it is the range of average effects that fit the data reasonably well. It is not:</p>
<ul>
<li>a guarantee the true value is inside this particular range</li>
<li>the range of results individual people got</li>
<li>a measure of whether the study was well designed</li>
</ul>
<p>Keep that last point in mind; we will come back to it.</p>
<h2 id="step-1-find-the-point-estimate-and-the-range">Step 1: Find the point estimate and the range</h2>
<p>Take STEP 1, the semaglutide 2.4 mg obesity trial. Average weight change at 68 weeks was -14.9% with semaglutide versus -2.4% with placebo. The difference was <strong>-12.4 percentage points (95% CI, -13.4 to -11.5)</strong>.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Point estimate:</strong> -12.4. The single best guess.</li>
<li><strong>Range:</strong> -13.4 to -11.5. Every value in there is a plausible average difference. All of them are big.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="step-2-check-the-width">Step 2: Check the width</h2>
<p>Width tells you precision, and precision mostly comes from how many people (or events) a study had.</p>

<p>Same drug, almost the same point estimate, but STEP 5&#39;s range is nearly three times wider because it enrolled about a sixth as many people. Both say semaglutide beats placebo substantially. STEP 1 just says it with more precision.</p>
<h2 id="step-3-check-whether-the-range-crosses-no-difference">Step 3: Check whether the range crosses &quot;no difference&quot;</h2>
<p>For a difference in percentage points, &quot;no difference&quot; is <strong>0</strong>. For a ratio (hazard ratio, odds ratio, relative risk), &quot;no difference&quot; is <strong>1.0</strong>. A hazard ratio below 1 means fewer events in the drug group.</p>

<p>The primary endpoint&#39;s whole range sits below 1: compatible with anything from a 28% to a 10% lower risk, and not compatible with no effect. Cardiovascular death&#39;s range runs from 0.71 (meaningfully lower) to 1.01 (a hair higher). That does <strong>not</strong> prove semaglutide has no effect on cardiovascular death. It means this trial could not distinguish a benefit from no difference with confidence. The label says exactly that: superiority for cardiovascular death &quot;was not confirmed,&quot; and an effect on heart failure &quot;has not been established.&quot;</p>
<p>&quot;Not proven&quot; and &quot;proven not to work&quot; are completely different statements. The CI is how you tell them apart.</p>

1f:Tf33,<h2 id="what-about-very-dramatic-results">What about very dramatic results?</h2>
<p>Here is one from SURMOUNT-1&#39;s three-year analysis in people with prediabetes. Type 2 diabetes developed in 1.3% of the tirzepatide groups versus 13.3% on placebo: <strong>hazard ratio 0.07 (95% CI, 0.0 to 0.1)</strong>. A range that far from 1 says the difference is very unlikely to be chance.</p>
<p>But the CI cannot tell you how much that matters to a person. For that you need the absolute numbers (1.3% versus 13.3%), the population (adults with obesity <em>and</em> prediabetes) and the time frame (176 weeks). Pair this post with <a href="/blog/relative-vs-absolute-risk-glp-1/">relative vs absolute risk</a> and you have the whole toolkit.</p>
<h2 id="three-common-ci-mistakes">Three common CI mistakes</h2>
<p><strong>1. Treating the interval as your personal range.</strong> The STEP 1 CI of -13.4 to -11.5 describes the <em>average difference between groups</em>. Individuals were far more spread out: about half of the semaglutide group lost 15% or more, while some lost very little. (See <a href="/blog/average-weight-loss-vs-responder-rates/">average weight loss vs responder rates</a>.)</p>
<p><strong>2. Comparing two drugs by eyeballing their separate intervals.</strong> SURMOUNT-1&#39;s 15 mg dose had a 72-week change of -20.9% (CI -21.8 to -19.9); STEP 1&#39;s semaglutide group lost -14.9%. Those numbers came from different trials with different people. To compare drugs fairly you need a head-to-head trial with its own CI for the <em>difference</em>, as in SURMOUNT-5. Even within one trial, whether two intervals overlap is not a proper test of whether they differ. (<a href="/blog/why-glp-1-studies-differ/">Why studies of the same drug differ</a>.)</p>
<p><strong>3. Thinking a narrow CI means a trustworthy study.</strong> A CI only measures random error. If the study was biased, measured the wrong thing or studied people unlike you, a tight CI just means it is precisely answering a different question. Always check the endpoint too (<a href="/blog/glp-1-trial-endpoint-explained/">GLP-1 trial endpoints explained</a>).</p>
<h2 id="a-20-second-ci-check-you-can-use-anywhere">A 20-second CI check you can use anywhere</h2>
<p>When you see &quot;X (95% CI, A to B)&quot;:</p>
<ol>
<li>Is the whole range on the helpful side of 0 (or 1)?</li>
<li>Is it narrow or wide relative to the effect?</li>
<li>Would the low end of the range still matter to you?</li>
<li>Is this the primary endpoint, and in people like you?</li>
</ol>
<p>That last question is the one statistics can&#39;t answer for you. Your prescriber can help with that part.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="what-does-95-ci-stand-for">What does &quot;95% CI&quot; stand for?</h3>
<p>95% confidence interval: a range calculated so that, over many repeated studies, 95% of such ranges would capture the true average effect.</p>
<h3 id="what-does-it-mean-if-a-hazard-ratio-39-s-confidence-interval-includes-1">What does it mean if a hazard ratio&#39;s confidence interval includes 1?</h3>
<p>The data are compatible with no difference between groups. It does not prove there is no effect; it means the study could not confidently show one.</p>
<h3 id="why-do-small-glp-1-trials-have-wider-confidence-intervals">Why do small GLP-1 trials have wider confidence intervals?</h3>
<p>Fewer people (or fewer events) means more room for chance, so the range of plausible average effects is wider. STEP 5, with 304 people, had a range nearly three times wider than STEP 1 with 1,961.</p>
<h3 id="is-a-p-value-the-same-as-a-confidence-interval">Is a p-value the same as a confidence interval?</h3>
<p>They are related but not the same. A p-value tells you how surprising the data would be if there were no effect; a CI shows the range of plausible effect sizes, which is usually more useful.</p>
