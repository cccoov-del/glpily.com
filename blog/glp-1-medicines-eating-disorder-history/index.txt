15:Tfa2,<p>If you&#39;ve lived with an eating disorder, or a complicated relationship with food, restriction or the scale, the idea of a medicine that turns down appetite can bring up mixed feelings. Hope, maybe. Worry, maybe. Both are reasonable. Your history deserves care and respect, not a blanket rule that GLP-1 treatment is always right or always wrong for you.</p>
<h2 id="what-research-knows-and-doesn-39-t-so-far">What research knows (and doesn&#39;t) so far</h2>
<p>The honest answer is: not much yet, in either direction.</p>
<ul>
<li>A 2024 review in the <em>International Journal of Eating Disorders</em> concluded that evidence is limited. The authors noted it&#39;s possible GLP-1 medicines could worsen or contribute to eating-disorder symptoms, or interfere with eating-disorder treatment, but that more research is needed before firm conclusions can be drawn either way.</li>
<li>Researchers are also studying these medicines for binge eating. A 2026 systematic review found 12 small studies (mostly fewer than 75 participants) of liraglutide, semaglutide and dulaglutide, with reductions in binge eating reported. The authors stressed the small samples, mixed methods and short follow-up, and called for larger randomized trials. None of these medicines is approved in the US to treat binge eating disorder.</li>
</ul>
<p>So GLP-1 medicines aren&#39;t a proven treatment for eating disorders, and they&#39;re not a substitute for evidence-based eating-disorder care.</p>
<h2 id="why-your-history-matters">Why your history matters</h2>
<p>GLP-1 medicines can reduce appetite and change how food feels. For some people, that may make it harder to notice when they aren&#39;t eating enough, or it may echo old patterns of restriction. For others, quieter food thoughts may feel like relief (we explore that in <a href="/blog/food-noise-glp-1/">food noise on GLP-1s</a>). Because people respond so differently, the decision and the follow-up need to be individual.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-tell-your-care-team">What to tell your care team</h2>
<p>Share relevant history with the prescribing clinician, even if it feels private or embarrassing: past or current binge eating, restriction, purging, compensatory exercise, or distress around weighing. Helpful questions include:</p>
<ul>
<li>How will my nutrition, mood and eating patterns be monitored?</li>
<li>What signs would mean we should review the plan?</li>
<li>Can you coordinate with my therapist, psychiatrist or dietitian?</li>
</ul>
<p>The goal is to look after physical health and mental wellbeing together, not one at the expense of the other.</p>
<h2 id="be-wary-of-extreme-rules">Be wary of extreme rules</h2>
<p>A medicine is never a reason to skip meals on purpose, follow severe restriction, or punish yourself for eating. If tracking food, weight or doses starts to feel obsessive, shame-filled or compulsive, tell your care team and ask about alternatives. A simple log that supports appointments is very different from tracking that runs your day. Our piece on <a href="/blog/glp-1-relationship-with-food/">how GLP-1 treatment can change your relationship with food</a> looks at this more broadly.</p>
<h2 id="when-to-reach-out-for-help">When to reach out for help</h2>
<p>Get qualified support if you&#39;re purging, can&#39;t eat enough, are having loss-of-control eating episodes that cause distress, or feel intense anxiety about food or weight. Signs like fainting, a racing or irregular heartbeat, or severe weakness need urgent medical care. If you&#39;re in the US and in crisis or having thoughts of harming yourself, you can call or text 988 at any time.</p>
<p>Please don&#39;t stop psychiatric treatment or change a GLP-1 prescription without talking to the clinician involved.</p>
<p>A good treatment conversation isn&#39;t about labeling you a &quot;good&quot; or &quot;bad&quot; candidate based on one fact. It&#39;s about understanding your history, goals and risks, and putting the right support around you.</p>
