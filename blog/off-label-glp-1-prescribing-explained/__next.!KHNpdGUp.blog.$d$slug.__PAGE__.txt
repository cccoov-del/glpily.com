18:T1056,<p>Off-label GLP-1 prescribing means a clinician prescribes an FDA-approved medicine for a use, age group or dose that is not listed on its official label, such as prescribing Ozempic, which is approved for type 2 diabetes, to someone without diabetes for weight loss. It is legal and common in medicine, but FDA has not reviewed whether the drug is safe and effective for that particular use.</p>
<p>That last bit is the whole point. Off-label is not &quot;illegal&quot; and it is not &quot;unapproved drug.&quot; It is &quot;approved drug, unreviewed use.&quot;</p>
<h2 id="what-does-fda-actually-say-about-off-label-use">What does FDA actually say about off-label use?</h2>
<p>FDA&#39;s patient page calls it &quot;unapproved use of an approved drug.&quot; It gives three common forms: using a drug for a condition it is not approved to treat, giving it a different way, or giving it at a different dose. Health care providers generally may prescribe a drug for an unapproved use when they judge it medically appropriate for the patient. FDA also notes that, for the unapproved use, it &quot;has not determined that the drug is safe and effective.&quot;</p>
<p>Common reasons for off-label prescribing include having no approved option for a condition, or approved options not working for that person.</p>

<p>Notice something funny? The <em>same molecule</em> can be on-label under one brand and off-label under another. Semaglutide for weight management is on-label as Wegovy and off-label as Ozempic. Tirzepatide for weight is on-label as Zepbound and off-label as Mounjaro. Same active ingredient, different approved uses, different labels, and sometimes very different insurance treatment.</p>
<h2 id="what-else-counts-as-off-label">What else counts as off-label?</h2>
<p>Off-label is not only about the condition. Using an approved product <strong>in a way the label does not describe</strong> also counts, for example:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A dosing schedule not on the label.</strong> Tiny doses, stretched intervals or skipped titration steps. Social media calls some of this &quot;microdosing&quot;; see <a href="/blog/glp-1-microdosing-trend-evidence/">what the evidence says about GLP-1 microdosing</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Combinations the label advises against.</strong> The Wegovy and Zepbound labels both say using them with other semaglutide or tirzepatide products, or with any other GLP-1 receptor agonist, is not recommended.</li>
</ul>

2a:T104f,<h2 id="off-label-is-not-the-same-as-unapproved">Off-label is not the same as unapproved</h2>
<p>This is where headlines and ads blur things, so here is the clean version:</p>
<table>

<th>Category</th>
<th>Is the product FDA-approved?</th>
<th>Example</th>
</tr>
</thead>

<td>On-label</td>
<td>Yes, for this use</td>
<td>Zepbound for an adult with obesity</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Off-label</td>

<td>Ozempic prescribed for weight loss without diabetes</td>

<p>An off-label prescription still involves a real, FDA-approved product with a known manufacturer, quality controls and a label full of safety information. Compounded and research-use products skip some or all of that. FDA&#39;s July 2024 alert on compounded semaglutide described overdoses of 5 to 20 times the intended dose, partly from confusing &quot;units&quot; and milliliters. That is a very different risk picture from an approved pen used off-label. See <a href="/blog/compounded-glp-1-safety-red-flags/">compounded GLP-1 red flags</a> and <a href="/blog/research-use-only-peptides-glp-1/">why &quot;research use only&quot; matters</a>.</p>
<h2 id="does-insurance-cover-off-label-glp-1-prescriptions">Does insurance cover off-label GLP-1 prescriptions?</h2>
<p>Often not, and this is where off-label gets practical. Plans usually tie coverage to approved or &quot;medically accepted&quot; uses. Medicare Part D, for example, generally covers drugs for a &quot;medically accepted indication,&quot; which CMS&#39;s Part D manual defines as an FDA-labeled indication or one supported by citation in recognized drug compendia (AHFS or DRUGDEX). Commercial plans set their own rules, and a prior authorization for a diabetes medicine may ask your prescriber to document the diabetes diagnosis. So an off-label prescription might be completely legal and still land you with the full cash price. Check before you fill. (<a href="/blog/read-health-plan-formulary-glp-1/">Reading a formulary for GLP-1 medicines</a>.)</p>
<h2 id="what-to-ask-your-prescriber-about-off-label-use">What to ask your prescriber about off-label use</h2>
<p>FDA suggests a few questions, and we added two:</p>
<ul>
<li>&quot;What is this drug approved for?&quot;</li>
<li>&quot;What studies support using it for my situation?&quot;</li>
<li>&quot;Are there approved options, and how do the benefits and risks compare?&quot;</li>
<li>&quot;How will we monitor whether it is working and safe?&quot;</li>
<li>&quot;Will my insurance cover it for this use?&quot;</li>
</ul>
<p>A good answer is specific to you. It is not &quot;everybody&#39;s doing it.&quot; If you go ahead, keeping a log of doses and side effects (GLPily has a shot and pill log with reminders) gives you and your prescriber something concrete to review at follow-up.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="is-taking-ozempic-for-weight-loss-off-label">Is taking Ozempic for weight loss off-label?</h3>
<p>Yes, if you do not have type 2 diabetes. Ozempic&#39;s label covers adults with type 2 diabetes. Semaglutide is approved for weight management under the brand Wegovy.</p>
<h3 id="is-off-label-prescribing-legal">Is off-label prescribing legal?</h3>
<p>Yes. Clinicians may generally prescribe approved drugs for unapproved uses when they judge it medically appropriate.</p>
<h3 id="is-off-label-the-same-as-compounded">Is off-label the same as compounded?</h3>
<p>No. Off-label uses an FDA-approved product. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved and do not undergo FDA premarket review of safety, effectiveness or quality.</p>
