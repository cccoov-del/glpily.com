Full Cost of GLP-1 Treatment: 9 Costs Beyond the Prescription

The prescription is the headline, but the first year of GLP-1 treatment also brings visits, labs, supplies, groceries, clothes that stop fitting and time off. Here are nine costs to plan for, with a budget sheet.

The full cost of GLP-1 treatment is the medicine plus everything around it: prescriber visits, any lab tests your clinician orders, supplies like pen needles and a sharps container, changes to your grocery cart, clothes as your size changes, maybe a gym or trainer, and time off for appointments or rough side-effect days. None of it is huge alone. Together it can add a real chunk to year one. Here are nine costs to plan for, and a sheet to add them up.

One promise: no scary totals pulled from nowhere. Your numbers are yours, and the sheet at the end is built for them.

1. The medicine itself (and the dose ladder)

This is the big one, and it moves. Self-pay prices often climb as the dose goes up. From the manufacturers' own pages in October 2026:

  • Zepbound KwikPen or vials: $299 (2.5 mg), $399 (5 mg), $449 for 7.5–15 mg if you refill within 45 days; regular price up to $699
  • Wegovy pen: $349 for most doses ($199 for the first two months of starter doses for new patients through Dec 31, 2026); Wegovy HD $399
  • Wegovy pill: $149–$299 by dose
  • Foundayo pill: $149–$299 by dose (top doses $349 without a 45-day refill)

With insurance, it's your copay or coinsurance instead, which can swing a lot during the year. We work through that in covered vs affordable. Before paying any seller, compare the advertised price with the real bill.

Budget tip: price the dose you'll likely be on in three months, not just the starter dose.

2. Prescriber visits

Starting a GLP-1 means at least one visit, then follow-ups as the dose changes. Costs range from an office copay to a telehealth program fee. If a monthly membership is involved, check whether it includes follow-ups and messages or charges for each. Our telehealth appointment checklist lists what to ask before you pay.

3. Lab tests your clinician orders

Not everyone needs the same tests, and this isn't a list of what you should get. But if your clinician orders bloodwork before or during treatment, it's a cost. With insurance it may hit your deductible; without, ask for the cash price before the draw.

4. Supplies: needles, sharps container, cooler

  • Pen needles. Multi-dose pens like the Zepbound KwikPen use separate pen needles. Medicare's GLP-1 Bridge, for example, says pen needles aren't covered and must be bought separately.
  • Sharps container. The FDA says to put used needles in a sharps disposal container right away and to follow your local rules. Mail-back programs usually charge a fee; drop-off sites at pharmacies, clinics or health departments may be free.
  • Travel cooler or case if you travel with injectable medicine. See traveling with GLP-1 medication.

5. Groceries: a different cart, not necessarily a bigger one

Smaller appetites can mean smaller portions, but many people shift toward protein-rich foods, Greek yogurt, eggs, beans, fish, and drinks that make hydration easier. Some swaps cost more per item, and some weeks you'll spend less because you're eating less. Track it for a month before deciding it's a budget problem. Our GLP-1 grocery list and budgeting tips help keep it reasonable.

6. Clothes that stop fitting

The cost nobody warns you about until your jeans are holding on by faith. If your size changes, you may buy "bridge" pieces more than once. Thrift, swaps, tailoring and adjustable waistbands stretch the budget. More ideas in clothes shopping when your fit keeps changing.

7. Movement: gym, classes, a trainer, or just shoes

Strength training is often encouraged during weight loss to help protect muscle (see why strength training can matter). That can be free (bodyweight at home), cheap (resistance bands), or monthly (gym or trainer). Pick the version you'll actually use.

8. Time off and getting there

  • Appointments. Time away from work, plus parking, transit or mileage. For context, the IRS medical mileage rate for 2026 is 20.5 cents a mile (January–June) and 23.5 cents (July–December). That's the figure for deducting medical travel, not a reimbursement promise.
  • Side-effect days. Nausea and fatigue are common during dose increases for some people. If you have little sick leave, plan dose changes around your calendar and talk to your prescriber about timing. See why side effects can flare during dose escalation.

9. Tracking and support tools

A notebook is free. Apps range from free to subscription. Can an HSA or FSA cover any of this?

Some of it. IRS Publication 502 says prescribed medicines are includible medical expenses, and weight-loss program fees count only when the weight loss treats a specific disease diagnosed by a physician (such as obesity). Gym dues generally don't count, and diet food doesn't either. The details, and how to check with your plan administrator, are in our HSA and FSA explainer.

When the numbers don't work

Cost is one of the most common reasons people stop treatment (we dig into that in why people stop GLP-1 medicines). If the budget is tight, tell your prescriber before you change anything. They may know about a different product, form or program, and they can plan any change safely.

Common questions

How much does GLP-1 treatment cost per year?

It depends on the product, dose, insurance and extras. Self-pay medicine alone ranged from about $149 to $699 a month in October 2026 depending on product and dose. Add visits, supplies and lifestyle costs using the sheet above.

Do I need to buy needles for GLP-1 pens?

It depends on the pen. Multi-dose pens like the Zepbound KwikPen use separate pen needles. Ask your pharmacist what your specific product needs.

Can I use my HSA for a gym membership while on a GLP-1?

Usually not. The IRS says gym dues generally aren't medical expenses unless bought for the sole purpose of treating a specific physician-diagnosed disease. Check with your plan administrator.

Are clothes a medical expense?

No. Ordinary personal items like clothing aren't medical expenses under IRS rules.
