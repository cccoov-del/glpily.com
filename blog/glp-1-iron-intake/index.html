GLP-1s and Iron Intake: Keeping Your Energy Up with a Small Appetite

GLP-1 medicines can make it tough to eat enough iron-rich foods, leading to low energy. Here’s how to keep your iron up, even with a smaller appetite.

If you’re taking a GLP-1 medication and feeling more tired than usual, you’re not alone. Many people using GLP-1s find their appetite shrinks so much that getting enough iron becomes a real challenge—sometimes leading to low energy or even iron deficiency. Here’s how to keep your iron intake on track, even when small meals feel like plenty.

Why GLP-1s Can Make Iron Intake a Struggle

GLP-1 receptor agonists are known for cutting hunger and helping people eat less. Randomized controlled trials show these medicines can reduce daily food intake by 16% to 39%, with semaglutide in particular lowering energy intake by about 24% (PMC13237850, PubMed 28266779). While this appetite change can support weight management, it also means you may not eat enough to meet all your nutritional needs—especially for essential micronutrients like iron.

Unlike some weight loss surgeries, GLP-1s don’t cause malabsorption. But because people simply eat less, it’s easier to fall short on iron and other vitamins and minerals (Nature 2025).

How Common Is Low Iron on GLP-1s?

Several research studies point to a real risk:

  • In a cross-sectional study of people using GLP-1s or dual agonists, a large portion had iron intakes below the Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs) (PMC13362726).
  • Real-world insurance claims reveal that 1.6% of new GLP-1 users were diagnosed with iron deficiency anemia at 6 months, rising to 3.2% at 12 months (PMC12943190).
  • A 2026 review found that reduced dietary variety, frequent stomach upset, and rapid weight loss on GLP-1s all contribute to a heightened risk for low iron (PMC13316283).

While not everyone will develop a deficiency, the risk is higher if you already have heavy periods, are older, have had bariatric surgery, or have a history of low iron (PMC13516115).

Signs of Low Iron: What to Watch For

Iron is vital for making hemoglobin, which helps carry oxygen in your blood. Even mild iron deficiency can cause symptoms like:

  • Unexplained fatigue or feeling run-down
  • Weakness or dizziness
  • Pale skin
  • Hair loss
  • Trouble concentrating
  • Shortness of breath, especially with activity

If you notice these symptoms, especially if they’re new or worsening, reach out to your clinician. Fatigue can have many causes, but iron deficiency is one that can be checked with a blood test. For more on tiredness and GLP-1s, see afternoon-fatigue-on-glp-1.

Eating for Iron: Strategies for a Small Appetite

With a limited appetite, every bite counts. Here’s how to make your meals work harder for your iron needs:

1. Prioritize Iron-Rich Foods

  • Animal sources (heme iron, best absorbed):
    • Lean beef, chicken, turkey, pork
    • Canned tuna or salmon
  • Plant sources (non-heme iron):
    • Lentils, beans, chickpeas
    • Tofu, tempeh
    • Fortified cereals or oatmeal
    • Spinach, pumpkin seeds, quinoa

2. Structure Your Meals for Maximum Impact

  • Eat protein and iron-rich foods first before moving to fruits, veggies, and then carbs. This helps you get the most nutrition before feeling full (health.osu.edu).
  • Avoid drinking large amounts of fluid with meals, as this can make you feel full faster and limit iron intake (health.osu.edu).
  • Aim for small, frequent meals if you can’t finish larger ones. Iron needs can be met over the day, not just in one sitting (PMC13316283).

3. Enhance Absorption

  • Pair iron-rich foods with vitamin C: Bell peppers, strawberries, oranges, or tomatoes can boost iron absorption from plant foods.
  • Avoid calcium supplements or high-calcium foods at the same time as iron. Calcium can block iron absorption, so separate them by a few hours if you take both (goodrx.com).

4. Consider Fortified and Convenient Options

If your appetite is too small for large portions of meat or beans, look for:

  • Iron-fortified cereals, oatmeal, or nutrition bars
  • Ready-to-drink shakes with added iron
  • Small portions of hummus or nut butters with whole-grain toast

Tracking Iron Intake and Symptoms

Because GLP-1s suppress hunger cues, it can be easy to forget to eat or miss key nutrients. Keeping a simple log—either in a notebook or an app like GLPily—of what you eat, your energy levels, and any new symptoms can help you and your clinician spot patterns. For tips on tracking, see what-to-track-on-glp-1 and glp-1-tracker-app-what-to-look-for.

Should You Take a Supplement?

There’s no one-size-fits-all answer. In the absence of formal guidelines, some nutrition experts recommend a daily multivitamin with iron for people at higher risk of deficiency, especially if you’re eating less, have a history of low iron, or have other risk factors (Nature 2025).

However, it’s important not to start iron supplements without checking with your clinician—too much iron can also cause problems, and your needs may vary. Periodic lab checks (like hemoglobin and ferritin) are especially helpful for people at higher risk (PMC13516115).

What’s Still Uncertain?

  • There aren’t yet large clinical trials measuring exactly how GLP-1 medicines affect iron status over time.
  • Some research suggests GLP-1s may decrease iron absorption, but evidence is limited and not conclusive (webmd.com).
  • Ferritin (a marker of iron stores) can drop with weight loss due to less inflammation, not necessarily because of iron deficiency (onlinelibrary.wiley.com).
  • There’s no consensus yet on who should be screened for iron deficiency or how often.

When to Reach Out

If you notice new or worsening fatigue, dizziness, or other signs of low iron, let your clinician know. These symptoms can have many causes, and a blood test is the only way to know for sure if iron is low.

For more on tracking symptoms and preparing for appointments, see share-glp-1-tracking-report-with-clinician.

Bottom Line

GLP-1 medications can make it harder to get enough iron, simply because you’re eating less. By prioritizing iron-rich foods, eating with intention, and tracking your intake and symptoms, you can help keep your energy steady. If you have concerns, talking with your clinician—and sharing your nutrition and symptom log—can help you get the care you need.
