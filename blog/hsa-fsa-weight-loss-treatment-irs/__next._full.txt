15:T166e,<p>Yes, you can generally use an HSA or FSA for a <strong>prescribed</strong> GLP-1 medicine, because IRS Publication 502 counts prescribed medicines as medical expenses. The everything-else around weight loss is pickier. A weight-loss <strong>program</strong> counts only if it treats a specific disease diagnosed by a physician (the IRS names obesity, hypertension and heart disease as examples), while <strong>gym dues</strong> and <strong>diet food</strong> generally don&#39;t count. Your account administrator makes the call on each claim, so check with them before you swipe.</p>
<p>This is general information about IRS rules, not tax advice. For a big or unusual expense, a tax professional is worth the call.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-hsa-and-fsa-rules-decide-what-counts">How do HSA and FSA rules decide what counts?</h2>
<p>Both accounts pay for &quot;qualified medical expenses,&quot; which generally means the costs that would qualify for the medical expense deduction. IRS Publication 502 is the rulebook people point to. Its core test: an expense has to be <strong>primarily</strong> to treat or prevent a disease or condition, not merely good for general health.</p>
<p>Two practical twists:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>FSAs can be narrower.</strong> IRS Publication 969 says an FSA&#39;s qualified expenses are the ones <strong>specified in the plan</strong>. Your employer&#39;s FSA might cover less than the IRS allows.</li>
<li><strong>Your administrator decides your claim.</strong> They may ask for documents. The IRS doesn&#39;t pre-approve purchases.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="what-about-compounded-glp-1s-or-research-products">What about compounded GLP-1s or &quot;research&quot; products?</h2>
<p>Pub 502 talks about <strong>prescribed</strong> drugs and drugs that were <strong>legally</strong> imported. It doesn&#39;t give a special rule for compounded products, so ask your administrator before paying with your account. Separately from the tax question, the FDA stresses that compounded GLP-1 drugs aren&#39;t FDA-approved and aren&#39;t reviewed for safety, effectiveness or quality before sale. Products sold &quot;for research use only&quot; aren&#39;t prescriptions at all (see <a href="/blog/research-use-only-peptides-glp-1/">why that label matters</a>). Our guide to <a href="/blog/compounded-glp-1-safety-red-flags/">compounded GLP-1 red flags</a> explains the safety side.</p>
<h2 id="why-does-diagnosed-by-a-physician-matter-so-much">Why does &quot;diagnosed by a physician&quot; matter so much?</h2>
<p>Because it&#39;s the line between medical care and general wellness. The same group meetings can be a qualified expense for someone whose physician diagnosed obesity and is treating it, and not for someone joining to &quot;get in shape.&quot; When an expense is in that gray zone, administrators commonly ask for documentation from your clinician that links the expense to a diagnosed condition. Ask your administrator what form they want before you buy, not after.</p>

<h2 id="what-happens-if-i-use-my-hsa-for-something-that-doesn-39-t-qualify">What happens if I use my HSA for something that doesn&#39;t qualify?</h2>
<p>Publication 969 says an HSA withdrawal that isn&#39;t for qualified medical expenses is added to your income and <strong>may be hit with an additional 20% tax</strong>. There are exceptions, such as after age 65. FSA administrators generally deny or claw back ineligible claims. Either way, keep proof.</p>
<p>Also, no double-dipping: you can&#39;t deduct an expense on your tax return that you paid with tax-free HSA or FSA money.</p>
<h2 id="a-simple-records-habit">A simple records habit</h2>
<p>For each account purchase, keep:</p>
<ul>
<li><input disabled="" type="checkbox"> The <strong>receipt</strong> or pharmacy printout (drug name, date, amount)</li>
<li><input disabled="" type="checkbox"> The <strong>prescription</strong> or clinician documentation for gray-zone items</li>
<li><input disabled="" type="checkbox"> Any <strong>administrator approval</strong> email or claim decision</li>
<li><input disabled="" type="checkbox"> A note of <strong>what it was for</strong></li>
</ul>
<p>A folder in your email works fine. If you already log your doses in GLPily, your shot or pill history is a handy cross-check for refill dates when a receipt goes missing. The receipt is still what counts, though.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="can-i-use-my-hsa-to-pay-for-wegovy-or-zepbound">Can I use my HSA to pay for Wegovy or Zepbound?</h3>
<p>Generally yes, if it&#39;s prescribed. IRS Publication 502 includes prescribed medicines as medical expenses. Your HSA administrator may still ask for documentation.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-use-my-fsa-for-a-glp-1-if-it-39-s-only-for-weight-loss">Can I use my FSA for a GLP-1 if it&#39;s only for weight loss?</h3>
<p>A prescribed medicine is generally a qualified expense. But an FSA can cover only what the employer&#39;s plan specifies, so check your plan rules and ask the administrator.</p>
<h3 id="is-a-gym-membership-hsa-eligible-if-i-39-m-on-a-glp-1">Is a gym membership HSA-eligible if I&#39;m on a GLP-1?</h3>
<p>Usually not. The IRS says gym dues are for general health unless bought for the sole purpose of treating a specific physician-diagnosed disease, or for a prescribed treatment such as physical therapy.</p>
<h3 id="are-protein-shakes-fsa-eligible">Are protein shakes FSA-eligible?</h3>
<p>Generally no. The IRS treats diet food and beverages as substitutes for normal nutrition. Special foods qualify only in narrow cases, and only the cost above normal food.</p>
