1a:Te90,<p>GIP stands for glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide. It&#39;s a hormone released by K cells in the upper small intestine when you eat, and it helps tell the pancreas to release insulin when blood sugar is rising. Tirzepatide is designed to activate both the GIP receptor and the GLP-1 receptor, which is why it&#39;s called a &quot;dual agonist&quot; rather than a GLP-1-only medicine.</p>
<h2 id="how-is-gip-different-from-glp-1">How is GIP different from GLP-1?</h2>
<p>GIP and GLP-1 are both <strong>incretins</strong>, gut hormones that boost insulin release after a meal. Together they explain the &quot;incretin effect&quot;: your body releases more insulin when glucose arrives by mouth than when the same glucose is given into a vein. But they come from different cells (GLP-1 is mostly made by L cells further down the gut), act on different receptors and do somewhat different jobs:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>GLP-1</strong> stimulates glucose-dependent insulin release, lowers glucagon, slows stomach emptying and has well-studied effects on appetite and fullness.</li>
<li><strong>GIP</strong> also stimulates insulin release and is involved in how the body handles nutrients and fat. In type 2 diabetes, the pancreas responds much less to natural GIP than it does in people without diabetes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Partly because of that, GIP was a less obvious drug target than GLP-1, and researchers are still working out exactly how much the GIP part of tirzepatide contributes to its effects.</p>
<h2 id="does-the-second-target-explain-the-results">Does the second target explain the results?</h2>
<p>The mechanism helps explain why tirzepatide may behave differently from GLP-1-only medicines, but clinical trials, not the number of targets, tell us how well it works. The clearest comparison is SURMOUNT-5 (NEJM, 2025): 751 adults with obesity but without type 2 diabetes were randomized to the highest tolerated dose of tirzepatide (10 or 15 mg) or semaglutide (1.7 or 2.4 mg) weekly for 72 weeks. Average weight change was −20.2% with tirzepatide and −13.7% with semaglutide. The trial was open-label and funded by Lilly, and gastrointestinal side effects were the most common in both groups.</p>
<p>That&#39;s a meaningful result for that population and those doses. It isn&#39;t proof that &quot;two hormones always beat one,&quot; and individual responses vary widely. If you&#39;re on tirzepatide and progress feels slow, our piece on <a href="/blog/mounjaro-not-losing-weight/">Mounjaro and not losing weight</a> looks at what research says.</p>
<h2 id="is-every-tirzepatide-product-approved-for-the-same-thing">Is every tirzepatide product approved for the same thing?</h2>
<p>No, and the brand name matters. In the US:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mounjaro</strong> is approved to improve blood sugar control in adults with type 2 diabetes and, since August 2026, to reduce the risk of major cardiovascular events in adults with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk.</li>
<li><strong>Zepbound</strong> is approved for chronic weight management and for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity.</li>
</ul>
<p>Approvals and brand names differ in other countries. For a side-by-side of tirzepatide and semaglutide brands, see <a href="/blog/mounjaro-vs-ozempic/">Mounjaro vs Ozempic</a>.</p>
<h2 id="the-takeaway">The takeaway</h2>
<p>Knowing what GIP is makes drug news easier to follow, especially as newer medicines add even more targets. But biology isn&#39;t a shortcut to a personal decision. The useful questions are what the trials showed, which approved product fits your situation where you live, and how your clinician will monitor your response and side effects.</p>
