15:T1302,<p>GLP-1 medicines are changing how some households shop, and there&#39;s now real evidence for it. A large Cornell study of grocery receipts, published in December 2025, found that households spent about 5.3% less on groceries within six months of someone starting a GLP-1, with the biggest drops in snacks and sweets. But many bolder headlines about &quot;the end of snacking&quot; or a whole industry being transformed are still forecasts, not facts.</p>
<p>Here&#39;s how to tell the two apart, and what it means for your own cart.</p>
<h2 id="how-many-people-are-taking-glp-1s">How many people are taking GLP-1s?</h2>
<p>Enough that food companies are paying attention. In a KFF poll run in late October and early November 2025, <strong>18% of U.S. adults</strong> said they had ever taken a GLP-1 drug and <strong>12%</strong> said they were taking one at the time. That&#39;s up from KFF&#39;s May 2024 poll. Not all of them use it for weight loss: about 30% of users said they took it mainly to lose weight. Many others take it for diabetes or another condition.</p>
<p>In the Cornell team&#39;s own data, the share of U.S. households with at least one GLP-1 user rose from about 11% in late 2023 to more than 16% by mid-2024.</p>
<h2 id="what-did-the-big-cornell-grocery-study-find">What did the big Cornell grocery study find?</h2>
<p>The study, &quot;The No-Hunger Games,&quot; by Sylvia Hristakeva and Jura Liaukonyte of Cornell with Leo Feler of the market research firm Numerator, was published in the <em>Journal of Marketing Research</em> on December 18, 2025. It&#39;s useful because it uses <strong>actual purchase data</strong>, not just what people say they buy. The researchers linked survey answers about GLP-1 use with transaction records from a panel of about 150,000 U.S. households.</p>
<p>Key findings, as reported by Cornell:</p>

<p>Two more details matter:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>It lasted, but faded.</strong> Among people who kept taking the medicine, lower food spending lasted at least a year, though the drop got smaller over time.</li>
<li><strong>It reversed after stopping.</strong> About a third of users stopped during the study. After that, spending went back to about where it was before, and baskets got slightly less healthy, partly from more candy and chocolate.</li>
</ul>
<p>The authors were clear about a limit: the study can&#39;t fully separate the drug&#39;s effect from other changes people make when they start treatment, like a new diet plan. They pointed to appetite reduction as a likely key reason, given the clinical evidence and the reversal after stopping. If you want to understand why studies like this are useful but different from clinical trials, see <a href="/blog/real-world-data-vs-randomized-glp-1-trials/">real-world data vs randomized trials</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-have-food-companies-actually-done">What have food companies actually done?</h2>
<p>Some things are documented, with dates and press releases. Here are two clear examples:</p>
<p><strong>Nestlé&#39;s Vital Pursuit (announced May 21, 2024).</strong> Nestlé USA announced a line of frozen bowls, pizzas and sandwich melts described as &quot;a companion for GLP-1 weight loss medication users.&quot; The release said the 12 launch items would be high in protein, a good source of fiber, portioned for a smaller appetite and priced at a suggested $4.99 or less, with stores getting them in the fourth quarter of 2024.</p>
<p><strong>Conagra&#39;s Healthy Choice badge (announced December 12, 2024).</strong> Conagra said that starting in January 2025, 26 Healthy Choice meals would carry an &quot;On Track&quot; badge calling them &quot;GLP-1 Friendly.&quot; Its criteria were high protein, low calorie and a good source of fiber. One detail is easy to miss: Conagra said <strong>no changes were made to the meals themselves</strong>. The food stayed the same, and the packaging got a new label.</p>
<p>That&#39;s the core lesson. A product launch or a new badge proves a company <em>made a business decision</em>. It doesn&#39;t prove the product works better for people on GLP-1s, or that everyone on these medicines wants it.</p>

1c:T158d,<h2 id="what-does-glp-1-friendly-on-a-label-actually-mean">What does &quot;GLP-1 friendly&quot; on a label actually mean?</h2>
<p>Not much, legally. FDA regulations define certain nutrient content claims with exact rules. For example, a food can say &quot;high&quot; in a nutrient if one serving has 20% or more of the Daily Value, and &quot;good source&quot; if it has 10% to 19%. Meals and main dishes have their own rules. &quot;GLP-1 friendly&quot; isn&#39;t one of those defined terms. It&#39;s a marketing phrase, and each company decides what it means.</p>
<p>So when you see it:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Flip to the Nutrition Facts panel.</strong> Check protein, fiber, calories and serving size yourself.</li>
<li><strong>Look at the portion.</strong> Smaller portions can be handy if your appetite is smaller. They can also mean less food for the price.</li>
<li><strong>Compare the price per serving</strong> with a similar meal that doesn&#39;t have the badge.</li>
<li><strong>Remember what your body tells you.</strong> If a food sits badly, the badge doesn&#39;t change that.</li>
</ol>
<p>Our <a href="/blog/glp-1-grocery-list/">GLP-1 grocery list</a> and <a href="/blog/high-protein-meals-glp-1/">high-protein meals</a> guides are a good place to plan around food you&#39;ll actually enjoy, badge or no badge.</p>
<h2 id="which-glp-1-food-headlines-are-still-guesswork">Which GLP-1 food headlines are still guesswork?</h2>
<p>Here&#39;s a quick way to sort the claims you&#39;ll see:</p>
<table>

<tr>
<th>Claim</th>
<th>Evidence level</th>
</tr>

<td><strong>Supported</strong> by large purchase-data research</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>&quot;Snack and sweet purchases drop the most.&quot;</td>
<td><strong>Supported</strong> in the same study</td>

</tr>

<p>Forecasts aren&#39;t bad. They&#39;re just guesses with spreadsheets. They depend on things nobody knows yet, like how prices change, how many people get coverage and how many people stay on treatment. (Remember, about a third of users in the Cornell study stopped.) Our guide on <a href="/blog/read-glp-1-study-headline-five-minutes/">reading a GLP-1 study headline in five minutes</a> works for business news too.</p>
<h2 id="what-does-this-mean-for-you-at-the-grocery-store">What does this mean for you at the grocery store?</h2>
<p>Probably less than the headlines suggest, and that&#39;s good news. You don&#39;t need special &quot;GLP-1 food.&quot; People in the Cornell study bought a bit more yogurt and fresh fruit and fewer chips. That isn&#39;t a rule, but it matches what many people find: when you&#39;re eating less, you often want each bite to count.</p>
<p>A few practical ideas:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Expect your grocery bill to change.</strong> If you&#39;re buying less, you may have room in the budget for better-quality protein or produce. Our <a href="/blog/glp-1-budgeting-tips/">GLP-1 budgeting tips</a> can help.</li>
<li><strong>Shop for your actual appetite.</strong> Smaller packages and single servings can cut down on waste.</li>
<li><strong>Keep an eye on nutrition, not just calories.</strong> With a smaller appetite, protein and fiber matter. Your prescriber or a registered dietitian can help you set goals.</li>
<li><strong>Scan, don&#39;t guess.</strong> If you use GLPily, the food scanner gives meals a GLPily Grade so you can compare options quickly. Use it as a guide, not gospel.</li>
</ul>
<p>The food industry story is worth watching. Just keep two piles: what has been measured, and what is being predicted.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="do-people-on-glp-1s-really-spend-less-on-food">Do people on GLP-1s really spend less on food?</h3>
<p>The best evidence so far says yes, on average. A Cornell study published in December 2025 found households with a GLP-1 user cut grocery spending by about 5.3% within six months, with bigger drops in snacks and sweets.</p>
<h3 id="what-does-glp-1-friendly-mean-on-food-packaging">What does &quot;GLP-1 friendly&quot; mean on food packaging?</h3>
<p>It&#39;s a marketing phrase, not a term defined in FDA regulations. Each company sets its own criteria. Conagra&#39;s version, for example, meant high protein, low calorie and a good source of fiber. Always check the Nutrition Facts panel.</p>
<h3 id="do-i-need-special-food-while-taking-a-glp-1">Do I need special food while taking a GLP-1?</h3>
<p>No. Regular foods that give you enough protein, fiber and fluids are generally the focus. Ask your prescriber or a registered dietitian about goals that fit your health.</p>
<h3 id="does-food-spending-go-back-up-if-you-stop-a-glp-1">Does food spending go back up if you stop a GLP-1?</h3>
<p>In the Cornell study, yes. After people stopped the medicine, their household food spending moved back toward pre-treatment levels.</p>
