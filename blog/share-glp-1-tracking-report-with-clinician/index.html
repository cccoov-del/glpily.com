Sharing GLP-1 Tracking Data With Your Doctor: Make It Useful

Your clinician has minutes, not hours. Research on patient-tracked data shows short summaries and trend graphs get used, while data dumps get skimmed. Here is how to build a report that earns its place in the visit.

To share GLP-1 tracking data with your doctor in a way that actually helps, bring one page built around one question: the date range, your medication history as taken, the trend that matters, symptoms with dates, and clear labels on anything estimated. Clinicians use short summaries and trend graphs; they skim screenshots.

That is not a guess. It is roughly what research on "patient-generated health data" keeps finding.

What do clinicians actually find useful in patient tracking data?

A systematic review in Health Informatics Journal (Lordon and colleagues, 2020) pulled together 13 studies on how patient-tracked data affects the patient-clinician relationship. The useful bits, in plain terms:

  • Clinicians did use patient data to adjust treatment plans and to understand what happened between visits.
  • Brief summaries reviewed before the appointment fit into their workflow and saved time.
  • Trend graphs and summary measures helped them make sense of the data.
  • Having the patient verbally summarize the data cut down the effort during the visit.

And the barriers: short appointments, no time to review data, and information overload.

So the goal is not "show everything." It is "make the important thing obvious in under a minute."

Start with the question, not the data

Before you export anything, finish this sentence: "At this visit, I want to figure out…"

  • "...whether the nausea after each dose increase is normal for me or worth changing something."
  • "...why my weight has been flat for six weeks."
  • "...whether I can manage the dose schedule around night shifts."

Your question decides what goes in. A nausea question needs symptom timing next to dose dates. A plateau question needs the weight trend and maybe measurements. Neither needs your water intake for the past four months.

What should a GLP-1 tracking report include?

Here is a structure that fits on one page:

Section What to put there Why it helps
Your question One or two sentences Sets the agenda in five seconds
Date range "Aug 1 to Oct 20" Clinicians need the window
Medication as taken Name, strength, dates of each change, missed or late doses The most important context for everything else
Main trend One graph or a few numbers (start, now, direction) Trends beat daily values
Symptoms What, when it started, how often, how bad, what helped Timing against dose dates is what clinicians look for
Other changes New medicines or supplements, illness, travel, cycle, stress Explains outliers
Estimates and gaps "Food values from photo estimates," "no weights Sept 3 to 14" Keeps the data honest

Notice what is not in the table: daily calorie totals, every journal entry, 40 screenshots. If your clinician wants more, they will ask, and you will have it.

How should you present weight and symptom trends?

Weight: show the direction, not the noise. Daily weight jumps around for many reasons (Why Your Weight Changes From Day to Day). A weekly average or a simple line over the period is far more readable than 80 single readings.

Symptoms: pair them with dose dates. "Nausea most days for the first 4 days after moving up to the next dose on Sept 2, easing by Sept 20" is gold. "Felt sick sometimes" is not. Our guide to tracking GLP-1 side effects shows how to log symptoms so they summarize well.

Medication: record what you actually took. If you were prescribed weekly doses but missed one, or took one two days late, put that in. It is not a confession; it is data your clinician needs to interpret everything else.

Label estimates and missing data

Tracking apps mix measured, entered and estimated numbers. A report should keep them apart:

  • Measured: your scale weight, a blood pressure cuff reading.
  • Entered: symptoms, doses, notes.
  • Estimated: food nutrition from photos, step-based calorie burn, and especially any estimated medication-level curve, which is a model, not a lab value. (GLP-1 Medication Level Charts explains why.)

Then say where the gaps are. "I stopped logging food in September" is more useful than a report that quietly averages the missing weeks.

How do you send tracking data to your doctor?

Ask the office which route they prefer. Common options:

  • The patient portal message with a PDF or short summary attached. Often the easiest for the clinic to file.
  • A printed page at check-in.
  • Apple Health's sharing feature, if your health system participates. In the US, the Health app can share selected categories (such as activity, heart rate, blood pressure, sleep and labs) with participating organizations; the data is encrypted end to end, and your provider views it inside their records system. You choose the categories, and you can stop sharing at any time.

A few safety habits:

  • Do not post reports to public links or social media, even "unlisted."
  • Check what the export contains. Some include your name, email, birth date or photos. Remove what is not needed.
  • Urgent symptoms do not wait for a report. Severe abdominal pain, signs of an allergic reaction, or vomiting you cannot keep fluids down are reasons to call your prescriber or 911, not to compile a summary.

A worked example

Here is what a useful report might say, in full:

Question: Is the stomach upset after dose increases something to adjust, or wait out? Range: Aug 4 to Oct 20. Medication: Weekly injection, Mondays. Dose increased Aug 4 and Sept 1. One dose taken Wednesday (Sept 15) because of travel. Weight: 214 to 203 lb, steady downward trend; flat Sept 8 to 22. Symptoms: Nausea days 1 to 3 after each increase, worst after Sept 1 (5 of 7 days). Constipation most of September. Better by Oct. Other: Started iron supplement Sept 5. Travel Sept 13 to 17. Estimates/gaps: Food from photo estimates. No weights during travel.

That is about 90 words. A clinician can read it in under a minute and immediately see the questions worth asking (the iron supplement and the constipation, for example).

Common questions

Should I bring my tracking app to a doctor's appointment?

Bring a summary, and have the app ready in case your clinician wants details. Scrolling through an app together eats time that is better spent on your question.

How much tracking data should I share with my doctor?

Usually one page: your question, the date range, medication as taken, the main trend, symptoms with dates and any gaps. Offer more if they ask.

Do doctors want to see my food logs?

Sometimes, for a specific question like protein intake or blood sugar patterns. Ask first. A one-line summary ("about 70 g protein most days, estimated") is often enough.

Is it safe to email my health data to my doctor?

Ask your clinic for its preferred secure route; a patient portal message is often the safest choice. Avoid public share links and check exports for personal details you do not need to send.
