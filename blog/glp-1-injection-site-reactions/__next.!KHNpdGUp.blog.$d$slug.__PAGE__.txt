1b:Tc2e,<p>Some injectable medicines can cause local reactions such as redness, itching, tenderness, bruising or a small area of swelling where the injection was given. How often this happens depends on the product: in the US prescribing information, injection site reactions were reported by 6–8% of people on Zepbound versus 2% on placebo, and by 1.4% on Wegovy versus 1% on placebo. Not every skin change after an injection is caused by the medicine. Follow the device instructions and official patient leaflet for your actual prescription rather than a social-media demonstration.</p>
<h2 id="record-the-appearance-and-timing">Record the appearance and timing</h2>
<p>If a reaction occurs, note when it appeared, how large it became, whether it is warm or painful, whether it is spreading, and how long it lasts. A photo can help show a clinician how the area changes over time, and a simple <a href="/blog/how-to-track-glp-1-shots/">shot log</a> makes it easy to see which site and date it followed. Avoid repeatedly scratching or applying unverified remedies. Do not reuse needles or share injection devices, and follow the medicine&#39;s and device&#39;s instructions for safe disposal.</p>
<h2 id="local-irritation-versus-a-possible-allergy">Local irritation versus a possible allergy</h2>
<p>A small, short-lived local reaction is different from a serious allergic reaction. US labels note that severe allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis and angioedema, have been reported with these medicines. Seek emergency medical help for trouble breathing, swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, faintness, or a widespread severe rash. Contact a clinician promptly for spreading redness, increasing pain, pus, fever, blistering, or a reaction that persists or gets worse. A healthcare professional can decide whether another cause, including infection, needs assessment.</p>
<h2 id="does-a-reaction-mean-the-injection-was-done-wrong">Does a reaction mean the injection was done wrong?</h2>
<p>Not necessarily. Skin sensitivity, technique, product factors and other causes can contribute. If you are unsure about technique, ask a pharmacist, nurse or prescriber to show you using the exact pen or device you have. Product instructions specify where to inject (for example, the Zepbound label lists the abdomen, thigh, or back of the upper arm if someone else injects) and say to rotate injection sites with each dose. Stick to those instructions, and do not inject into skin the official instructions say to avoid.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-bring-to-a-review">What to bring to a review</h2>
<p>Share the product name, lot number if available, injection date, site, timing, a photo and any other symptoms. Mention allergies and previous reactions. If your medicine came from a compounding pharmacy or an unfamiliar online source, say so; it changes what your clinician needs to consider (see <a href="/blog/compounded-glp-1-safety-red-flags/">compounded GLP-1 red flags</a>). A precise record helps the clinician decide whether simple observation, a technique review or a change to the treatment plan is appropriate.</p>
