18:Tdc3,<p>Before-and-after images can be motivating, discouraging or both. The problem is not that people share their progress; it is that a photograph usually leaves out the context needed to understand it. You may not know how long someone has been in treatment, what else changed, whether the images were taken under the same conditions, or whether the person had side effects or interruptions along the way.</p>
<h2 id="a-photo-is-not-a-clinical-outcome-report">A photo is not a clinical outcome report</h2>
<p>Lighting, pose, clothing, camera angle, time of day and image selection can all change how a body appears. A pair of images also cannot tell you about blood pressure, glucose, sleep, physical function, nutrition or emotional wellbeing. Even a completely genuine story is not a prediction of what will happen to someone else.</p>
<h2 id="trial-averages-and-personal-stories-answer-different-questions">Trial averages and personal stories answer different questions</h2>
<p>Randomized trials estimate outcomes across groups of people with specific eligibility criteria and follow-up. In the STEP 1 trial, for example, adults with obesity or overweight and without diabetes who took semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly alongside lifestyle support lost 14.9% of body weight on average over 68 weeks, compared with 2.4% on placebo. But that average hides a wide spread: about half of semaglutide participants with week-68 data lost 15% or more, while roughly one in seven lost less than 5%.</p>
<p>So a dramatic photo may show someone at the far end of that range, and a trial average still cannot tell you exactly where you will land. Researchers are still working out why responses vary; a 2026 systematic review linked lower response to factors such as baseline metabolic health, sex, age, adherence and possibly genetics, while noting that the findings need validation. (We dig into this in <a href="/blog/why-some-people-respond-less-to-glp-1/">why some people respond less to GLP-1 medicines</a>.)</p>
<h2 id="what-stories-often-leave-out">What stories often leave out</h2>
<p>The wait for appointments, the cost of medication, nausea, supply problems, changes in appetite, family support and reasons someone might stop treatment can all be missing from a short post. In a 2026 interview study of 30 US adults who had used GLP-1 medicines (most of them with type 2 diabetes), participants described a wide range of benefits and challenges, from quieter food thoughts to side effects serious enough to stop treatment, plus cost and access barriers. That variety matters: there is no single “correct” GLP-1 journey.</p>
<h2 id="a-kinder-way-to-use-social-media">A kinder way to use social media</h2>
<p>Ask whether a post is selling something, whether its claims are backed by sources, whether the person describes the timeframe, and whether it promises a specific result. Curate feeds that leave you informed rather than ashamed. If comparing yourself to pictures makes you anxious or pushes you toward unsafe restriction, take a break, and tell someone you trust or your clinician.</p>
<p>Your own data tells a more useful story than a stranger&#39;s: trends over weeks, how you feel, and measures beyond the scale (see <a href="/blog/how-to-track-glp-1-progress/">how to track GLP-1 progress beyond the scale</a>). A story can inspire a question, but it should not become a personal medical plan. Your treatment deserves to be judged against your health, goals and experience—not someone else&#39;s edited photo.</p>
