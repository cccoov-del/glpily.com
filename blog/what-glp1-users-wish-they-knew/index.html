What GLP-1 Users Describe: 6 Things Worth Knowing Before Starting

A research-based look at side effects, food noise, stigma, cost and support gaps GLP-1 users describe—and what new users can ask.

Online, GLP-1 journeys are often reduced to before-and-after photos. Real treatment is more complicated: a new relationship with hunger, digestive symptoms, appointments, cost, unexpected benefits, uncertainty and questions that don't fit neatly into a progress picture.

A 2026 qualitative study in JAMA Network Open interviewed 30 people who were using or had used GLP-1 receptor agonists. The sample was small and cannot tell us how common each experience is across all users. Participants were US adults (mean age 54), and 27 of the 30 had type 2 diabetes, so findings may differ for people prescribed these medicines for weight management alone. Still, it offers a useful window into the issues people want healthcare teams to understand.

1. The change may be mental as well as physical

Some interviewees described less psychological hunger, fewer cravings or a quieter stream of thoughts about food. Others had different experiences. “Food noise” is a popular term for these experiences, not a formal diagnosis, and it doesn't mean everyone taking a GLP-1 will feel the same change.

It's okay to talk about this with a clinician. Appetite and food thoughts are part of quality of life, not just a private test of willpower.

2. Side effects aren't a measure of success

The interviews included a wide spectrum: some participants described few significant problems, while others experienced digestive symptoms that became difficult enough to stop treatment. Some were willing to tolerate unpleasant effects because they felt the benefits mattered so much.

That willingness is understandable, but a severe or persistent adverse effect shouldn't be treated as proof that a medicine is working better. Ask in advance what symptoms are expected, what can be managed, and when to contact the prescriber.

3. The practical details can be as hard as the medicine itself

Cost, supply, appointment access and varying levels of clinical support came up in the study. A treatment plan can look straightforward on paper and become much harder when a person has to find a pharmacy, cover the expense, understand a symptom or fit changes into family life.

If access or side effects make continuing difficult, tell the care team. Those are legitimate treatment issues, not a personal shortcoming.

4. Stigma still shapes what people feel safe saying

Some participants worried that others would view medication as “taking the easy way out” or judge them differently depending on whether the prescription was for diabetes or weight management. This can make people hide a treatment that is meant to be part of healthcare.

You don't owe everyone a detailed explanation of your medication. If you choose to tell people, it can help to decide in advance what you do and don't want to discuss.

5. The medicine isn't the entire support system

Participants described GLP-1 treatment as something that could make other changes feel more possible, rather than replacing all effort or care. Clear explanations of side effects, ongoing clinician support, behavioral tools and a sense of connection can all matter.

It doesn't mean everyone needs the same plan. It means the prescription should be part of a conversation, not the whole conversation.

6. Other people's stories can help, but they aren't instructions

Participants in the study valued connecting with others who were using these medicines. Peer discussions can make someone feel less alone. They can also intensify comparison: one person loses quickly, another sees little change, and someone else stops because of cost or side effects. Online accounts rarely show every relevant detail, and they cannot tell an individual what dose to take or whether to switch medication.

Use stories to find questions worth asking—not as a substitute for a healthcare professional who understands your circumstances.

Questions worth asking before starting

  • What benefits should we measure besides weight?
  • Which side effects are common, and which need medical attention?
  • What should I do if I struggle to eat or drink enough?
  • How will we review response and tolerability?
  • What happens if cost or supply becomes a problem?
  • What support is available for long-term maintenance?

A timely safety update: mental-health headlines need context

In January 2026, the US FDA said its review found no increased risk of suicidal ideation or behavior with GLP-1 receptor agonist medicines, based on a meta-analysis of 91 placebo-controlled trials (107,910 patients) and other data, and requested removal of the relevant warning language from the labels of Saxenda, Wegovy and Zepbound. This update matters, but it does not mean that a person can never experience mood changes during treatment, that every product label is identical, or that new or worsening mental-health symptoms should be ignored. Anyone in distress should seek support, and medication questions belong with a qualified clinician. As of October 2026, the US Wegovy and Zepbound labels list this warning as removed; labels outside the US may differ. FDA still advises anyone with depression, suicidal thoughts or unusual mood changes to tell their healthcare provider. In the US, call or text 988 for immediate support.

About the study behind this article

The interviews were published in JAMA Network Open on 5 June 2026 by researchers at Stanford. They held video interviews with 30 US adults from 15 states between July and September 2025: mean age 54; 19 women, 10 men and 1 nonbinary participant; 27 of the 30 had type 2 diabetes. Twenty-three were still using a GLP-1 medicine and seven had stopped.

The researchers grouped what people said into eight themes in two areas:

  • Benefits and trade-offs: reduced food noise, psychological hunger or appetite; a sense that GLP-1s are not a standalone solution; a wide range of side effects; and a willingness to put up with side effects and logistical hurdles.
  • Social, clinical and structural context: stigma shaped by whether the prescription was for diabetes or weight; information and clinical support that felt essential but varied widely; access and cost barriers; and the value of sharing experiences with others.

Interviews like these surface questions large trials aren't designed to answer, but they aren't a survey: they can't tell you what percentage of all users experience something, prove that a medicine caused a particular change, compare products, or predict how you'll respond.

The takeaway

The human story of GLP-1 treatment includes quieted cravings and meaningful health changes for some people, alongside side effects, uncertainty, stigma and access barriers. Good information should make room for all of those experiences without promising a particular result.
