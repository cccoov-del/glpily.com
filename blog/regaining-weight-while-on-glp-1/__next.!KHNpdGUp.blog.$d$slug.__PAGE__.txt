19:Tc71,<p>Seeing your weight go up while you&#39;re still taking a GLP-1 can be discouraging. A short-term rise can come from fluid, constipation, menstrual-cycle timing, travel, a salty meal or different weighing conditions rather than a change in body fat. A steady upward trend deserves a calm review, but one reading can&#39;t explain what happened.</p>
<h2 id="start-by-separating-a-fluctuation-from-a-trend">Start by separating a fluctuation from a trend</h2>
<p>If weighing is part of your plan, use consistent conditions and look at several weeks rather than reacting to a single day. A <a href="/calculators/weight-trend-calculator/">weight trend calculator</a> can help smooth out the noise, and here&#39;s more on <a href="/blog/why-weight-changes-day-to-day/">why weight changes from day to day</a>. Note any recent constipation, illness, changes in activity or sleep, a new medicine, or a stretch of disrupted routine.</p>
<p>Some changes shouldn&#39;t be treated as ordinary scale noise. Rapid, unexplained weight gain, especially with new swelling in the legs or belly or shortness of breath, can have medical causes such as heart or kidney problems. Contact a clinician promptly, and get urgent care if breathing is difficult.</p>
<h2 id="plateau-slow-regain-or-something-else">Plateau, slow regain or something else?</h2>
<p>It&#39;s normal for weight loss to slow and level off over time; in long trials, average weight loss typically flattens into a plateau rather than continuing indefinitely (see <a href="/blog/glp-1-weight-loss-plateau/">GLP-1 weight-loss plateaus</a>). A plateau is different from steady regain, and both are worth describing accurately to your prescriber.</p>
<p>A 2026 systematic review of low weight-loss response highlights factors such as time on treatment, consistency of treatment, and individual metabolic characteristics like insulin resistance. Real-world treatment can also be interrupted by cost, insurance or supply problems, and missed doses or gaps matter: a large BMJ review found that weight tends to come back after weight-management medicines are stopped. Some other medicines are also linked to weight gain. None of this is about willpower, and the exact medicine and its purpose matter.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-do-next">What to do next</h2>
<p>Don&#39;t respond by starving yourself, over-exercising, taking extra medicine or adding a second drug. Share the trend with your prescriber, including your current plan, any gaps, side effects and health changes. If you take diabetes medicines, don&#39;t change them on your own because your weight shifted. Your clinician can decide whether further evaluation or a change in treatment makes sense.</p>
<h2 id="look-beyond-the-scale">Look beyond the scale</h2>
<p>Where relevant, look at symptoms, glucose results, blood pressure, mobility or other markers you&#39;ve agreed on, alongside weight. Progress isn&#39;t defined by one number, but a sustained change shouldn&#39;t be brushed off without a review either.</p>
<p>The question isn&#39;t &quot;How do I punish this increase away?&quot; It&#39;s &quot;What could explain this pattern, and what&#39;s the safest next step?&quot;</p>
