18:T1330,<p>A <strong>medication list for urgent care</strong> should fit in your wallet and on your phone&#39;s lock screen. If you take a GLP-1, it needs four things: the exact product, the strength, how often you take it, and when you last took it. The FDA recommends that everyone keep a current medication list with them at all times, including a photo on your phone or a paper copy next to your insurance card, because in an emergency it helps clinicians even when you can&#39;t answer questions.</p>
<p>This guide is about unplanned visits, like urgent care, the ER or an ambulance ride. Planning for a scheduled surgery is a separate job with its own <a href="/blog/glp-1-preop-medication-checklist/">pre-op checklist</a>.</p>
<h2 id="why-your-glp-1-matters-at-urgent-care">Why your GLP-1 matters at urgent care</h2>
<p>Picture it: it&#39;s 11 p.m., you have sharp upper-belly pain and you&#39;ve vomited three times. The triage nurse asks, &quot;Any medications?&quot; You say &quot;just my blood pressure pill.&quot; The pen in your fridge doesn&#39;t come to mind because you only use it on Sundays.</p>
<p>That missing detail matters. The prescribing information for GLP-1 medicines lists warnings that overlap with common reasons people end up at urgent care. The Zepbound label, for example, has warning sections for:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Severe gastrointestinal reactions</strong> (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea)</li>
<li><strong>Acute kidney injury from fluid loss</strong>, linked to vomiting and diarrhea</li>
<li><strong>Acute gallbladder disease</strong></li>
<li><strong>Acute pancreatitis</strong></li>
<li><strong>Hypoglycemia</strong>, especially alongside insulin or sulfonylureas</li>
<li><strong>Pulmonary aspiration under general anesthesia or deep sedation</strong>, relevant if the ER needs to sedate you</li>
</ul>
<p>&quot;Weekly GLP-1, strength increased two weeks ago, last dose Sunday&quot; helps the clinician decide what to check first. It doesn&#39;t mean the medicine caused your problem. It means they get to consider it.</p>
<p>There&#39;s also a timing reason. These medicines stay in your body for days. The tirzepatide label gives a half-life of about five days, so &quot;I stopped yesterday&quot; doesn&#39;t mean it&#39;s gone. The label also notes that slower stomach emptying can affect how other oral medicines are absorbed, which matters if the visit ends with a new prescription.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-put-on-an-emergency-medication-list">What to put on an emergency medication list</h2>
<p>The FDA&#39;s consumer guidance says to include emergency contacts, allergies and, for every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, vitamin and supplement, its <strong>name, strength, what it&#39;s for, and when and how you take it.</strong> For a GLP-1, add:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Brand and ingredient</strong>, e.g., &quot;Mounjaro (tirzepatide).&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Strength and form</strong>: &quot;5 mg weekly injection&quot; or &quot;14 mg daily tablet.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Usual dose day and last dose date.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Date of the last strength increase.</strong> Side effects are more common during dose escalation.</li>
<li><strong>Brand or compounded.</strong> If compounded, write the concentration printed on the vial. Compounded products aren&#39;t FDA-approved, and units can be confusing.</li>
<li><strong>Other glucose-lowering medicines.</strong> Insulin plus a GLP-1 is a combination clinicians will want to know about.</li>
</ul>
<p>Skip what doesn&#39;t help in an emergency: your goal weight, your pharmacy coupon, the story of your insurance appeal.</p>

2a:T1331,<h2 id="how-to-put-your-medication-list-on-your-phone">How to put your medication list on your phone</h2>
<p>A paper card can get left in another bag. A lock-screen entry can&#39;t, as long as you have your phone.</p>
<p><strong>iPhone (Health app Medical ID)</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Open <strong>Health</strong> and tap your <strong>profile picture</strong> at the top.</li>
<li>Tap <strong>Medical ID</strong>, then <strong>Edit</strong>.</li>
<li>Fill in fields such as <strong>Medications</strong>, <strong>Allergies</strong> and <strong>Conditions</strong>, and add <strong>Emergency Contacts</strong>.</li>
<li>Turn on <strong>Show When Locked</strong>. Apple says this lets responders see your Medical ID from the Lock Screen without your passcode.</li>
<li>Optionally turn on <strong>Share During Emergency Call</strong> so your Medical ID is sent automatically when you call or text emergency services (not available in every region).</li>
</ol>
<p>Someone helping you can reach it from the Lock Screen by tapping <strong>Emergency</strong>, then <strong>Medical ID</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Android (Safety app)</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Open the <strong>Safety</strong> app (called Personal Safety on some phones).</li>
<li>Tap <strong>Your info</strong>, then <strong>Medical information</strong>.</li>
<li>Add <strong>medications</strong>, allergies and other details.</li>
<li>Under <strong>Emergency info access</strong>, choose <strong>Show when locked</strong>.</li>
</ol>
<p>To view it: swipe up on the lock screen, tap <strong>Emergency</strong>, then <strong>View emergency info</strong>. Menu names vary by phone maker and Android version.</p>
<p><strong>A privacy note:</strong> both Apple and Google point out that lock-screen info is visible to <strong>anyone</strong> holding your phone. You decide how much to show. &quot;GLP-1 medicine (semaglutide), weekly&quot; is enough for a responder. If you share a phone or prefer to keep it private, the wallet card may suit you better (see our post on <a href="/blog/glp-1-app-shared-phone-privacy/">GLP-1 apps on a shared phone</a>).</p>
<p>GLPily&#39;s shot log keeps your dose dates and strength changes in one place, which makes updating the card quicker. It doesn&#39;t replace the card or your phone&#39;s Medical ID, because nobody at urgent care will open a tracking app.</p>
<h2 id="keep-the-list-current-three-triggers">Keep the list current: three triggers</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Strength changes.</strong> Update the card the day your new pen arrives.</li>
<li><strong>Any new prescription or supplement.</strong> The FDA specifically says to update the list whenever you start, change or stop a medicine.</li>
<li><strong>Before travel.</strong> A trip is when you&#39;re most likely to end up at an unfamiliar clinic. Our <a href="/blog/traveling-with-glp-1-medication/">travel guide</a> covers the rest of the packing list.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="when-to-skip-urgent-care-and-call-911">When to skip urgent care and call 911</h2>
<p>Urgent care is for problems that can&#39;t wait but aren&#39;t life-threatening. Call 911 for trouble breathing, chest pain, fainting, confusion, or severe symptoms that are getting worse quickly. For a suspected medication overdose or dosing error, GLP-1 labels point to the <strong>Poison Help Line, 1-800-222-1222</strong>. Our post on <a href="/blog/wrong-dose-glp-1-what-to-do/">taking the wrong amount</a> explains what happens next. For severe vomiting or abdominal pain, see our guides to <a href="/blog/vomiting-on-glp-1-warning-signs/">vomiting warning signs</a> and <a href="/blog/pancreatitis-glp-1-medicines/">pancreatitis</a>.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="do-i-need-to-list-my-glp-1-if-the-urgent-care-visit-seems-unrelated-like-a-sprained-ankle">Do I need to list my GLP-1 if the urgent care visit seems unrelated, like a sprained ankle?</h3>
<p>Yes. You may need pain medicine, a new prescription or sedation to set a bone, and clinicians check every new prescription against your current medicines. Listing everything every time is the habit that protects you.</p>
<h3 id="is-a-photo-of-my-pen-or-box-good-enough">Is a photo of my pen or box good enough?</h3>
<p>It&#39;s a decent backup. The FDA mentions a smartphone photo as one option. But a photo won&#39;t show your last dose date or other medicines, so a written list is better.</p>
<h3 id="should-my-list-say-why-i-take-the-glp-1">Should my list say why I take the GLP-1?</h3>
<p>The FDA recommends noting what each medicine is for. &quot;Type 2 diabetes&quot; vs &quot;weight management&quot; can change how a clinician reads your blood sugar or symptoms.</p>
<h3 id="how-often-should-i-review-the-card">How often should I review the card?</h3>
<p>Whenever a medicine changes, and at least each time you see your prescriber. Write the date at the top so anyone reading it knows how current it is.</p>
