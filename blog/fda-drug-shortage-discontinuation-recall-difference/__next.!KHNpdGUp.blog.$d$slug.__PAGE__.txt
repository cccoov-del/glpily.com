18:T19e2,<p>A <strong>drug shortage</strong> means national supply can&#39;t meet demand. A <strong>discontinuation</strong> means a manufacturer is permanently stopping a product. A <strong>recall</strong> means a specific product, usually specific lots, is being pulled or corrected because something is wrong with it. They sound similar at the pharmacy counter, but each one calls for a different next step.</p>
<p>GLP-1 medicines have given us real examples of all three in the past few years. Here&#39;s the plain-English version of each, using FDA&#39;s own definitions.</p>
<h2 id="what-is-a-drug-shortage-officially">What is a drug shortage, officially?</h2>
<p>Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, FDA defines a shortage as &quot;a period of time when the demand or projected demand for the drug within the United States exceeds the supply of the drug.&quot; Two details matter:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>It&#39;s national.</strong> FDA judges shortages at the US level. Its FAQ notes that when a single pharmacy is out of stock, that&#39;s usually a temporary distribution problem, not a national shortage.</li>
<li><strong>It&#39;s temporary by design.</strong> A shortage gets marked <strong>&quot;Resolved&quot;</strong> when FDA&#39;s Drug Shortages Staff determines that supply from at least one manufacturer covers total market demand. Some strengths or pack sizes can still be spotty after that.</li>
</ul>
<p>FDA&#39;s FAQ says manufacturing and quality problems are the most common cause. Other causes include delays, raw-material problems and sudden spikes in demand. The last one is very familiar to anyone who tried to fill a GLP-1 prescription in 2022–2024.</p>
<p><strong>GLP-1 example: tirzepatide and semaglutide.</strong> Both injections spent long stretches on FDA&#39;s shortage list during the demand surge. FDA&#39;s compounding-policy page records the endings:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>October 2, 2024:</strong> FDA determined the tirzepatide injection shortage was resolved. After litigation sent the decision back for review, FDA <strong>reaffirmed it on December 19, 2024</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>February 21, 2025:</strong> FDA determined the semaglutide injection shortage was resolved. FDA said Novo Nordisk&#39;s supply was meeting or exceeding demand, while warning that people might still see &quot;intermittent and limited localized supply disruptions.&quot;</li>
</ul>
<p>Notice that last phrase. &quot;Resolved&quot; doesn&#39;t mean every pharmacy has every strength every day.</p>
<p><strong>A shortage still running:</strong> As of late October 2026, FDA&#39;s database lists <strong>liraglutide injection</strong> as &quot;Currently in Shortage.&quot; It was first posted July 18, 2023, and Victoza pens are shown with limited availability due to a shipping delay. To check the latest status yourself, see <a href="/blog/check-current-glp-1-shortage-status/">how to check current GLP-1 shortage status</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-is-a-discontinuation">What is a discontinuation?</h2>
<p>A discontinuation is a <strong>business or manufacturing decision to stop making a product for good</strong>. It isn&#39;t a safety finding. Under section 506C of the FD&amp;C Act, makers of certain important drugs must notify FDA of a permanent discontinuance, generally at least <strong>6 months in advance</strong>, or as soon as practicable. FDA posts these notices in a separate &quot;Discontinuations&quot; tab and notes that the listings reflect what manufacturers report.</p>
<p><strong>GLP-1 examples, both from FDA&#39;s database:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Saxenda (liraglutide 3 mg for weight management):</strong> FDA&#39;s shortage entry lists Novo Nordisk&#39;s Saxenda pens as &quot;available until discontinuation in January 2027,&quot; with the reason given as discontinued manufacture. Liraglutide itself isn&#39;t disappearing, though. The same entry lists generic liraglutide pens from other companies as available.</li>
<li><strong>Original Rybelsus tablets (3, 7 and 14 mg):</strong> FDA&#39;s discontinuation listing, posted June 4, 2026, gives the reason as &quot;discontinuation of the manufacture of the drug,&quot; and says the products will be replaced by Ozempic (semaglutide) tablets.</li>
</ul>
<p>That pattern is common. A brand or strength goes away while the active ingredient stays available in another form. That makes a discontinuation a <em>prescription conversation</em>, not a reason to panic-buy. If you&#39;re switching between liraglutide and semaglutide products, <a href="/blog/semaglutide-vs-liraglutide/">this comparison</a> covers how they differ.</p>
<h2 id="what-is-a-recall-and-what-do-class-i-ii-and-iii-mean">What is a recall, and what do Class I, II and III mean?</h2>
<p>FDA describes recalls as &quot;actions taken by a firm to remove a product from the market.&quot; Most are voluntary. A company can also recall at FDA&#39;s request, or under an FDA order where the law allows it. Every recall gets a class based on risk:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Recall class</th>
<th>FDA&#39;s meaning in plain words</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td><strong>Class I</strong></td>
<td>Reasonable probability the product could cause serious health problems or death</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Class II</strong></td>
<td>May cause temporary or medically reversible health problems, or serious harm is unlikely</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Class III</strong></td>
<td>Not likely to cause health problems, but breaks FDA rules</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Market withdrawal</strong></td>
<td>A minor issue FDA wouldn&#39;t take legal action over; the firm removes or fixes it</td>
</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>Recalls are <strong>specific</strong>. They name products and usually lot numbers, which is why your first move is always to compare the lot on your box.</p>
<p><strong>GLP-1 example: compounded vials.</strong> In an April 2026 warning letter, FDA notes that ProRx LLC, a 503B outsourcing facility, voluntarily recalled a lot of compounded tirzepatide and various lots of compounded semaglutide multidose vials on October 15, 2025, &quot;due to a lack of sterility assurance.&quot; That recall touched specific compounded products. It didn&#39;t touch brand-name pens.</p>
<p>One more wrinkle: not every recall gets a press release. FDA&#39;s recalls page says plainly that &quot;not all recalls have press releases or are posted on this page.&quot; FDA&#39;s Enforcement Reports are the fuller record. Our guide to <a href="/blog/read-fda-safety-alert-glp-1/">reading an FDA safety alert</a> walks through what each part of a notice means.</p>

2a:Te6e,<h2 id="counterfeits-are-a-fourth-thing">Counterfeits are a fourth thing</h2>
<p>A counterfeit isn&#39;t a recall of a real product. It&#39;s a <strong>fake</strong> product posing as a real one. When FDA found counterfeit Ozempic in the US supply chain, it seized the fakes and published lot and label clues. Genuine Ozempic wasn&#39;t recalled. We cover those cases in <a href="/blog/counterfeit-glp-1-medicines-online-offers/">counterfeit GLP-1 medicines</a>.</p>

<h2 id="what-not-to-do-in-any-of-the-three">What not to do in any of the three</h2>
<p>Don&#39;t fill a gap with an unfamiliar online seller, split or stretch doses on your own, or switch products without your prescriber. FDA&#39;s shortage FAQ points out that it doesn&#39;t regulate the practice of medicine. Choosing an alternative treatment is your prescriber&#39;s call. If you miss doses during a supply gap, your label has specific instructions. See <a href="/blog/missed-glp-1-dose-instructions/">missed GLP-1 dose instructions</a> and ask your prescriber before restarting.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="is-a-discontinued-drug-the-same-as-a-recalled-drug">Is a discontinued drug the same as a recalled drug?</h3>
<p>No. A discontinuation is a decision to stop making a product, usually for business reasons, with advance notice. A recall pulls specific products because of a defect, contamination or labeling problem.</p>
<h3 id="if-fda-says-a-shortage-is-resolved-why-can-39-t-my-pharmacy-get-it">If FDA says a shortage is &quot;resolved,&quot; why can&#39;t my pharmacy get it?</h3>
<p>&quot;Resolved&quot; means national supply from at least one manufacturer covers demand. Specific strengths, pack sizes or local pharmacies can still have gaps. FDA warned of &quot;intermittent and limited localized supply disruptions&quot; when it ended the semaglutide shortage.</p>
<h3 id="is-saxenda-being-discontinued">Is Saxenda being discontinued?</h3>
<p>FDA&#39;s shortage database lists Novo Nordisk&#39;s Saxenda pens as available until discontinuation in January 2027. Generic liraglutide pens from other manufacturers are listed as available. Ask your prescriber about next steps.</p>
<h3 id="which-recall-class-is-the-most-serious">Which recall class is the most serious?</h3>
<p>Class I. It covers products with a reasonable probability of causing serious health problems or death.</p>
