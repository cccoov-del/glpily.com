18:T120c,<p>If you think you took too much of a GLP-1 medicine, or the wrong dose, call the Poison Help line at <strong>1-800-222-1222</strong>, your prescriber or your pharmacist now, even if you feel fine. If someone has trouble breathing, collapses, has a seizure or cannot be woken up, call <strong>911</strong>.</p>
<p>That is straight from the labels. The Zepbound Medication Guide, for example, says that if you take too much, call your healthcare provider or the Poison Help line, or go to the nearest emergency room right away. The rest of this page is about making that call quick and useful, and about what not to do while you wait.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-glp-1-dose-mistakes-happen">How do GLP-1 dose mistakes happen?</h2>
<p>Usually in ordinary, very human ways:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The &quot;did I already?&quot; double.</strong> Weekly shots are easy to lose track of. You inject Sunday, forget, and inject again Tuesday.</li>
<li><strong>The wrong pen.</strong> You start a new, higher-strength pen but still have the old one in the fridge, and grab the wrong one.</li>
<li><strong>The vial mix-up.</strong> With compounded vials, a dose written in &quot;units&quot; gets drawn up as mL, or a new vial has a different concentration. The FDA has documented people injecting <strong>5 to 20 times</strong> their intended dose this way. Our explainer on <a href="/blog/mg-ml-units-injectable-medication-confusion/">mg, mL and units</a> walks through why.</li>
<li><strong>The pill slip.</strong> With daily oral options, taking two in one morning.</li>
<li><strong>Someone else&#39;s medicine.</strong> A partner&#39;s pen looks like yours.</li>
</ul>
<p>None of these makes you careless. They are the reason pharmacists ask so many questions.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-do-in-the-next-10-minutes-a-checklist">What to do in the next 10 minutes: a checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Check for emergency signs first.</strong> Trouble breathing, fainting, seizure, confusion or not waking up means <strong>911</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Call for advice, don&#39;t search for it.</strong> Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) is free, expert and confidential, and is the number printed in the overdose section of GLP-1 labels. Your prescriber or pharmacist works too. If you are outside the US, use your local poison center or emergency number.</li>
<li><strong>Have the product in your hand.</strong> The pen, vial, carton or pharmacy label.</li>
<li><strong>Answer these questions</strong> (jot them down while you dial):</li>

<h2 id="what-not-to-do">What not to do</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Don&#39;t skip or split the next dose to &quot;even it out&quot;</strong> unless the person advising you says so. Whether and when to take the next one is their call.</li>
<li><strong>Don&#39;t force yourself to vomit</strong> or take home remedies.</li>
<li><strong>Don&#39;t wait for symptoms before calling.</strong> The Medication Guide says to call &quot;right away&quot; if you take too much, not once you feel sick.</li>
<li><strong>Don&#39;t rely on a forum timeline</strong> of what someone else felt. Their product, dose and body were different.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="why-effects-can-last-for-days">Why effects can last for days</h2>
<p>GLP-1 medicines are built to last. The labels give the half-life of semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) as about <strong>one week</strong>, and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) as about <strong>five days</strong>. The overdosage sections of these labels say a longer period of observation and treatment may be needed for exactly that reason.</p>
<p>In practice, that means a mistake on Sunday can still be showing up on Thursday. The advice you get may include watching for symptoms over several days, not just tonight.</p>

2a:T13d0,<h2 id="which-symptoms-need-help-and-how-fast">Which symptoms need help, and how fast?</h2>
<p>The overdosage section of the Wegovy label lists <strong>severe nausea, severe vomiting and severe hypoglycemia</strong> as effects reported with GLP-1 overdoses. FDA&#39;s dosing-error reports for compounded semaglutide also included abdominal pain, fainting, headache, dehydration, pancreatitis and gallstones.</p>

<p>The hypoglycemia signs above are from the Zepbound Medication Guide, which also lists dizziness, sweating, confusion or drowsiness, headache, hunger, weakness, anxiety and feeling jittery. The labels note the risk of low blood sugar is higher when a GLP-1 is combined with insulin or medicines like sulfonylureas. For more, see <a href="/blog/glp-1-medicines-low-blood-sugar/">can GLP-1 medicines cause low blood sugar?</a> and <a href="/blog/vomiting-on-glp-1-warning-signs/">vomiting on a GLP-1: when to worry</a>.</p>
<h2 id="missed-dose-vs-extra-dose-not-the-same-problem">Missed dose vs. extra dose: not the same problem</h2>
<p>People sometimes reach for the missed-dose rules after a double dose. Those are different instructions for a different situation. If what actually happened is that you&#39;re <strong>not sure</strong> whether you injected, that still counts as a &quot;call and ask&quot; moment. Our <a href="/blog/missed-glp-1-dose-instructions/">missed-dose guide</a> covers what each label says when a dose was skipped.</p>
<h2 id="after-the-scare-make-it-less-likely-next-time">After the scare: make it less likely next time</h2>
<p>Once things have settled:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ask your pharmacist to walk through your device or vial</strong> with you. FDA advises patients to ask their provider or compounder how to measure and give the intended dose, and asks providers to supply a syringe sized for it.</li>
<li><strong>Store only your current pen in reach.</strong> Put old strengths back with the pharmacy for disposal or in a separate, labeled spot.</li>
<li><strong>Log the dose the moment you take it.</strong> A shot log with reminders (GLPily has one, alongside a symptom log) answers &quot;did I already?&quot; in two seconds and gives the next clinician a clean timeline.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a current medication list</strong> on your phone or in your wallet. Our <a href="/blog/glp-1-urgent-care-medication-list/">urgent care medication list</a> shows what to include.</li>
<li><strong>Report it.</strong> The FDA collects reports of medication errors and side effects through MedWatch, online or by fax at 1-800-FDA-0178. Patterns in those reports are how the compounded-semaglutide dosing alert came about. See <a href="/blog/report-glp-1-side-effect-medwatch/">how to report a suspected GLP-1 side effect</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="i-accidentally-took-a-double-dose-of-ozempic-should-i-skip-next-week">I accidentally took a double dose of Ozempic. Should I skip next week?</h3>
<p>Don&#39;t decide that alone. Call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222), your prescriber or your pharmacist; they will tell you how to handle the next dose based on your product and what happened.</p>
<h3 id="can-you-overdose-on-a-glp-1-medicine">Can you overdose on a GLP-1 medicine?</h3>
<p>Yes. The labels&#39; overdosage sections say overdoses have been reported, with effects including severe nausea, severe vomiting and severe low blood sugar, and that longer observation may be needed because these drugs last a long time in the body.</p>
<h3 id="should-i-go-to-the-er-if-i-feel-fine">Should I go to the ER if I feel fine?</h3>
<p>Call Poison Help or your prescriber first unless you have emergency signs. They can tell you whether you can stay home and what to watch for, or whether to go in. Call 911 for trouble breathing, collapse, seizures or someone who can&#39;t be woken.</p>
<h3 id="is-it-safe-to-make-myself-throw-up-after-taking-too-much">Is it safe to make myself throw up after taking too much?</h3>
<p>No. Don&#39;t induce vomiting or try home remedies unless the person advising you tells you to.</p>
