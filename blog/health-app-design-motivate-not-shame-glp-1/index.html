Health App Design That Motivates, Not Shames: 5 Principles

Red numbers, broken streaks and 'bad food' labels can make a tracker feel like a disappointed teacher. Here is our opinion on five design principles for health apps that motivate GLP-1 users without shame.

Health app design should motivate without shaming: describe what happened instead of judging it, explain what every score can and cannot know, and let people skip, hide or turn off anything that feels like pressure. This is an opinion piece from the team that builds GLPily, a GLP-1 tracker, so read it as our view on what good design looks like, backed by the research that shaped it.

We have a stake in this. We build an app with a food grade and reminders, so we spend a lot of time asking whether a screen helps someone or just makes them feel watched.

Why does shame in health apps matter?

Because weight stigma is not a minor feeling. In 2020, a panel of international experts published a consensus statement in Nature Medicine on ending the stigma of obesity. Among its conclusions: weight-based stigma and internalized weight bias can be particularly harmful to mental health, and fear of prejudice can keep people from getting care. The signatories pledged to avoid stereotypical language, images and narratives. They also noted that over two thirds of images accompanying US media reports on obesity contain weight stigma.

Apps are media too. Every icon, color and notification is a small piece of language.

And tracking has two sides. A classic systematic review (Burke and colleagues, 2011) found a consistent association between self-monitoring and weight loss, though the evidence was weak by modern standards. Meanwhile, a 2017 study of 105 people with eating disorders found that about three quarters had used a popular calorie-tracking app, and most of those said it had contributed to their symptoms. That is self-reported, from a specific group, and does not prove cause, but it is a clear signal that the same feature can help one person and hurt another. (GLP-1 Medicines and Eating-Disorder History explores this further.)

Good design takes both seriously.

Principle 1: Describe the data, never grade the person

Compare:

Shaming Descriptive
"You failed your streak!" "You logged 4 days this week."
"Bad choice" "Lower in protein than your other meals today"
"Weight up 2.4 lb 😟" "Weight 2.4 lb higher than yesterday. Daily changes are common."
"You're off track" "Here's your 4-week trend."

The descriptive column is not softer for the sake of it. It is more accurate. A missed log might be a busy day, a holiday, a deliberate break or a privacy choice. A single weigh-in reflects water, salt and timing as much as anything (Why Your Weight Changes From Day to Day).

Any food score should grade the food, explain what it looked at, and never imply anything about the person eating it. That is the standard we try to hold the GLPily Grade to, and it is why we wrote Can You Trust a Food-Photo Grade? about its limits.

Principle 2: Show uncertainty right next to the number

Health apps are full of estimates: calories from a photo, a predicted trend line, an estimated medication curve, a generated goal image. Each one looks more certain than it is.

Good design puts the caveat beside the number, not in a help page:

  • "Estimated from a photo. Check the portions."
  • "Estimate based on your logged doses, not a measurement."
  • "An illustration, not a prediction."

Uncertainty is not a legal footnote. It protects people from treating a guess as a verdict on their body, and it keeps an estimate from quietly becoming a reason to change medication. That decision belongs to the prescriber.

Principle 3: Reminders should invite, not scold

The tone of a notification matters because it arrives uninvited, often on a lock screen.

  • Invite: "Shot day. Log it when you're ready."
  • Scold: "You haven't logged in 3 days!"

A few design habits we think make a difference:

  • Let people choose which reminders they get and when.
  • Never use guilt to bring someone back ("We miss you…" with a sad face).
  • Keep health details off the lock screen unless the user opts in.
  • Make it as easy to pause reminders as it is to turn them on.

If your app's reminders have become background noise or a source of dread, it is fine to turn most of them off and keep only the medication one.

Principle 4: Make streaks, badges and comparisons optional

Gamification can be fun. It can also turn health into a scoreboard you are losing.

  • Streaks punish exactly the moments when life is hardest: illness, travel, grief, a heavy week at work.
  • Leaderboards and comparisons invite people to measure themselves against bodies, medicines and timelines that are not theirs. We wrote about that pull in When Friends Compare Every Pound.
  • Badges for weight milestones can feel great or feel like a deadline.

The fix is simple: make them optional, make them easy to switch off, and never take away data or features when someone stops "playing."

Principle 5: Let people control their own data

Shame often comes from feeling exposed. Control helps:

  • Correct anything. Wrong weight, wrong date, duplicate dose: one tap to fix.
  • Hide or skip categories. Not everyone wants to log food, weigh daily or take photos. Basic tracking should work without them.
  • Delete and export. Leaving should not mean losing your history or leaving it behind.
  • Privacy by default. Sensitive things, like progress photos, should be private unless the user chooses otherwise.

What you can do as a user today

You do not have to wait for apps to get better:

  1. Turn off what stings. Streaks, daily weigh-in prompts, calorie targets, badges.
  2. Change the weigh-in rhythm. Weekly may tell you the trend with less daily noise. Ask your clinician what is useful.
  3. Hide the numbers that hurt and keep the ones that help, like dose dates and symptoms.
  4. Notice your reaction. If opening the app brings dread, guilt or compulsive checking, that is useful information. Change the settings, take a break, or talk to your clinician, especially if you have a history of disordered eating.
  5. Change apps if needed. A tracker is a tool. If a tool hurts, use a different one, or paper.

And if you ever feel judged for being on a GLP-1 at all, by an app or a person, Being Judged for Using GLP-1 is for you.

Common questions

Can health tracking apps make you feel worse?

They can for some people, especially with judgmental language, streaks or rigid targets. Research on calorie-tracking apps in people with eating disorders found many felt the app contributed to their symptoms. If an app makes you feel worse, adjust it or stop using it, and talk to your clinician.

What makes a health app non-judgmental?

Neutral, descriptive language; clear labels on estimates; reminders you control; optional streaks and badges; and full control over what you log, hide, export or delete.

Should I stop tracking if it stresses me out?

Not necessarily. Many people do better tracking less: only doses and symptoms, or weighing weekly. Ask your clinician which data actually matters for your care.

Is self-monitoring helpful for weight management?

A systematic review found a consistent association between self-monitoring and weight loss, although the quality of the evidence was limited. Helpful for many, not required for everyone.
