What Is Wegovy HD 7.2 mg? Higher Dose, Evidence and Questions

Wegovy HD is a 7.2 mg weekly semaglutide dose approved in the US in March 2026. What trials found, side effects to know, and why it isn’t for self-adjusting.

Wegovy HD is a higher, 7.2 mg once-weekly dose of semaglutide injection, approved by the US FDA on March 19, 2026, for long-term weight reduction in adults with obesity, or overweight plus at least one weight-related condition. It adds another step for some people already on Wegovy, but the exact label, not an online thread, decides who it's for and how it's used.

Who it's meant for

Under the US label, the usual Wegovy maintenance dose for adults is still 2.4 mg (or 1.7 mg). The 7.2 mg dose is an option for adults who have tolerated 2.4 mg for at least 4 weeks when their clinician thinks more weight reduction is needed. It isn't a starting dose, and its safety hasn't been established in teens or for the MASH (fatty liver) indication. Approval and availability outside the US may differ.

What a higher dose does, and doesn't, mean

A higher dose is not a shortcut to try on your own. Prescription medicines are stepped up and maintained according to product-specific instructions meant to balance benefit and safety. Never split, combine or improvise doses with pens or vials to make up a different strength; that can lead to serious medication errors and side effects.

What the trials found

The label describes two 72-week randomized trials comparing 7.2 mg, 2.4 mg and placebo:

  • Adults with obesity, without diabetes (1,407 people): average weight change was −18.8% on 7.2 mg, −15.5% on 2.4 mg and −3.9% on placebo. That's about 3.3 percentage points more than 2.4 mg.
  • Adults with obesity and type 2 diabetes (512 people): −13.2% on 7.2 mg, −10.4% on 2.4 mg and −3.8% on placebo. The FDA noted blood-sugar control (HbA1c) was similar to the lower dose.

These are group averages from company-run trials. Individual results vary, and comparing them with numbers from other medicines' trials isn't the same as a head-to-head study.

What about tolerability?

The higher dose comes with some trade-offs worth knowing:

  • Altered skin sensations. Tingling, burning, skin sensitivity or pain (called dysesthesia) was reported by 22% of people on 7.2 mg, compared with 6% on 2.4 mg and 0.3% on placebo. About a quarter of those affected had their dose lowered and a few paused or stopped; most of them recovered, and symptoms tended to clear faster when the dose was adjusted. The FDA said it's continuing to look into this reaction.
  • Hair loss was reported in 5.8% on 7.2 mg, 3.3% on 2.4 mg and 1.0% on placebo, more often in women. Our guide to hair loss and muscle loss on GLP-1s explains what's known.
  • Digestive side effects like nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation and stomach pain remain the most common. About 5% of people on either 7.2 mg or 2.4 mg stopped treatment because of side effects, compared with 2% on placebo.

The full prescribing information also covers the boxed thyroid warning and other precautions such as pancreatitis and gallbladder problems. If you notice new skin sensations or digestive symptoms, tell your prescriber rather than changing the dose yourself.

What should someone ask?

Is this dose approved and available where I live? Does the evidence fit my situation? How will we track response and side effects? What are the alternatives? And try not to judge your own progress against someone else's; a slower result isn't a reason to change a dose on your own.

Wegovy HD is a development worth following, but a high-dose headline shouldn't turn into an unsupervised dosing plan. For how it fits with the other 2026 options, see the new weight-loss medicines of 2026.
