1a:T9b4,<p>Semaglutide and liraglutide are both GLP-1 receptor agonists, and both were developed by Novo Nordisk, but they aren&#39;t interchangeable. They differ in molecular structure, how long they last in the body, how often they&#39;re taken, which brands they&#39;re sold under, what they&#39;re approved for and what the trials showed.</p>
<h2 id="brands-and-routines-at-a-glance-us">Brands and routines at a glance (US)</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Liraglutide</strong> is a once-daily injection. Victoza is approved for type 2 diabetes (including reducing major cardiovascular events in adults with type 2 diabetes and established heart disease), and Saxenda is a higher-dose version approved for chronic weight management in adults and in adolescents aged 12 and older who meet weight criteria. Generic liraglutide is also available in the US: the FDA approved the first generic referencing Victoza in December 2024, and the first generic version of Saxenda launched in August 2025.</li>
<li><strong>Semaglutide</strong> lasts longer, so it can be taken once weekly by injection: Ozempic for type 2 diabetes and related heart and kidney indications, and Wegovy for weight management and other labeled uses. It also comes as daily tablets (Rybelsus and Ozempic tablets for type 2 diabetes, Wegovy tablets for weight). There&#39;s more on that in <a href="/blog/glp-1-pills-vs-injections/">GLP-1 pills vs injections</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>The same ingredient can carry different labels depending on the brand and dose, so the exact product name on your prescription matters.</p>
<h2 id="is-there-a-head-to-head-trial">Is there a head-to-head trial?</h2>
<p>Yes, which is unusual and useful. The STEP 8 trial (published in JAMA in 2022) randomized 338 adults with overweight or obesity <em>without</em> diabetes to weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg or daily liraglutide 3.0 mg (the weight-management doses), or placebo, for 68 weeks, alongside diet and activity counseling. Average weight change was:</p>
<ul>
<li>−15.8% with semaglutide</li>
<li>−6.4% with liraglutide</li>
<li>−1.9% with placebo</li>
</ul>
<p>More people stopped treatment for any reason on liraglutide (27.6%) than on semaglutide (13.5%), while gastrointestinal side effects were similarly common in both groups (84.1% and 82.7%). The comparison between the two medicines wasn&#39;t blinded, and results apply to this population and these doses. People with type 2 diabetes, or on diabetes doses, may see different results.</p>

2f:T4f3,<h2 id="side-effects-and-risks">Side effects and risks</h2>
<p>Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting and constipation are common across the class, especially as doses increase. Both medicines share important class warnings, but specific rates, warnings and approved benefits come from each product&#39;s own label. A benefit shown for one medicine, such as a heart or kidney outcome, shouldn&#39;t be assumed for another just because both act on the same receptor. Our guide to <a href="/blog/ozempic-side-effects/">Ozempic side effects</a> covers what&#39;s common and what&#39;s serious.</p>
<h2 id="questions-for-your-prescriber">Questions for your prescriber</h2>
<p>Why is one being suggested over the other for me? Which outcome matters most in my case: blood sugar, weight, heart or kidney health? Does a daily or weekly routine fit better? What do coverage and cost look like, including generics? Which side effects should prompt a call? And please don&#39;t switch, combine or adjust these medicines on your own, because doses don&#39;t convert directly between them.</p>
<p>There&#39;s no single &quot;best GLP-1&quot; for everyone. The useful comparison is product-specific, weighing evidence, approved uses, safety, routine and your own health context together.</p>
