18:T786,<p>&quot;Is it working?&quot; is a fair question, but it needs a defined outcome and timeframe. A GLP-1 medicine might be prescribed for chronic weight management, while a related product might be prescribed for type 2 diabetes or another approved use. How treatment is judged should reflect the specific product, why it was prescribed, its label and your clinician&#39;s plan.</p>
<h2 id="one-weigh-in-can-39-t-answer-the-question">One weigh-in can&#39;t answer the question</h2>
<p>Weight shifts with water, bowel contents, the menstrual cycle, sleep, activity and more. The trend over a reasonable period tells you far more than any single number. Some health outcomes can improve without the scale matching exactly, and side effects and tolerability still matter even when weight is going down.</p>
<h2 id="how-response-has-been-defined">How response has been defined</h2>
<p>Researchers and guidelines often express weight loss as a percentage of starting weight rather than pounds or kilograms, because 5 kg means something different at 80 kg than at 150 kg. (You can work it out with the <a href="/calculators/weight-loss-percentage/">weight-loss percentage calculator</a>.) Trials commonly report how many people reached at least 5%, 10%, 15% or 20% loss, since losses of around 5% or more have been linked with improvements in measures such as blood sugar and blood pressure in many people.</p>
<p>Some guidelines have used early checkpoints. A 2015 Endocrine Society guideline recommended assessing weight-loss medicines after about three months and considering stopping if weight loss was under 5%. That guideline was written before semaglutide and tirzepatide were approved for weight management, and today&#39;s medicines use dose escalation over several months, so prescribers may time reviews differently. It&#39;s a useful example of how &quot;response&quot; gets defined, not a rule to apply to yourself.</p>

2b:T67f,<h2 id="what-clinicians-may-review">What clinicians may review</h2>
<p>A review may include your treatment timeline, the prescribed regimen, any interruptions, the weight trend, glucose markers when relevant, blood pressure, symptoms, other medicines, practical access and your personal goals. A trial&#39;s average percent weight change is useful background, not a promise. Real-world research also shows that side effects, insurance coverage and staying connected with a prescriber influence whether people remain on treatment at all.</p>
<h2 id="why-there-39-s-no-universal-online-threshold">Why there&#39;s no universal online threshold</h2>
<p>Different products and labels set different expectations and instructions. An internet rule like &quot;everyone should lose X pounds by week Y&quot; ignores individual context and can push people toward unsafe self-adjustment. Your prescriber can explain the timeline and criteria that apply to your medicine.</p>
<h2 id="make-the-visit-useful">Make the visit useful</h2>
<p>Before an appointment, write down what you hoped treatment would improve, what has changed, what hasn&#39;t, and what&#39;s been difficult. Ask when response will be formally reviewed and what options would be considered if the plan isn&#39;t meeting the goal. A <a href="/blog/three-month-glp-1-check-in/">three-month check-in</a> is a natural moment for this. Don&#39;t increase the dose, stop, or combine medicines without guidance.</p>
<p>A response isn&#39;t a dramatic before-and-after photo. It&#39;s a meaningful change judged alongside safety, tolerability, daily function and the goals you&#39;ve agreed with your care team.</p>
