GLP-1 Influencer Sponsorship Disclosure: How to Follow the Money

A creator can be sincere and still be paid. Here is what U.S. rules expect a GLP-1 sponsorship disclosure to look like, the quiet ways money shows up, and a quick check before you click 'shop now'.

To evaluate a medical influencer's GLP-1 sponsorship disclosure, check whether the relationship is stated clearly and early in the content itself (for example "ad," "sponsored" or "paid partnership with [brand]"), what kind of benefit the creator gets (payment, free product, affiliate commission, discount code, equity or a job), and whether the health claims match the official label. Under FTC guidance, a disclosure hidden in a bio, buried after "more," or written as "collab" is not good enough.

Sponsorship does not make someone wrong. Doctors, dietitians and patients with sponsors can share useful, accurate information. But money is context, and with prescription medicines and telehealth offers, context matters a lot.

What do U.S. rules say influencers must disclose?

The FTC's "Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers" is short and surprisingly readable. The core points:

  • Disclose any financial, employment, personal or family relationship with a brand. That includes free or discounted products. Compensation does not have to be money.
  • Disclose even if you think you are unbiased, and even if the brand did not ask you to mention that specific product.
  • Put it where people will see it. Not only on a profile page, at the end of a post, or behind a "more" link.
  • In videos, say it in the video, not just in the description. In live streams, repeat it.
  • Use plain words like "ad," "sponsored," or "thanks to [brand] for the free product." Vague tags like "sp," "collab" or "thanks" do not cut it.
  • Do not rely only on a platform's built-in disclosure tool.
  • Do not make health claims you cannot back up with proper scientific support.

Since 2024, the FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials also bans AI-generated or otherwise fake testimonials, undisclosed reviews from company insiders, and buying fake followers or views to misrepresent influence, with civil penalties for knowing violations.

Why are GLP-1 posts under extra scrutiny?

Because prescription drug promotion has its own rules. On September 9, 2025, FDA announced a crackdown on deceptive drug advertising, sending thousands of letters to drug companies and about 100 cease-and-desist letters. FDA's announcement singled out undisclosed paid influencer promotion and social media for blurring the line between editorial content, user posts and drug ads. It also cited a 2024 review finding that every pharmaceutical social media post studied highlighted benefits, but only 33% mentioned potential harms.

That 33% figure is a useful mental check. If a GLP-1 post is all upside, the missing half is probably not an accident. (For what FDA has said specifically to telehealth sellers, see FDA warning letters to telehealth companies.)

The quiet ways money shows up

A big "Paid partnership" label is the easy case. The trickier ones:

What you see What it can mean
"Use my code SLIM20" The creator often earns a commission or credit on each purchase
"Link in bio" to a telehealth or supplement shop Possibly an affiliate link that pays per sign-up
"I've been using this for months" about a brand's program Possibly free or discounted treatment, which counts as compensation
"Our clinic" or "my program" Ownership or employment; the creator profits directly
An "independent" review site with one obvious winner Could be company-controlled; the FTC rule bans misrepresenting these as independent
Huge follower count, oddly generic comments Possibly bought engagement, which the FTC rule also targets

None of these prove bad faith. They tell you to weigh the post as marketing, not as a neutral second opinion.

A 6-point money-trail check

Run this in a minute before acting on a GLP-1 post:

  1. Is there a clear disclosure in the first seconds or first lines? If you had to dig, note that.
  2. What exactly is being sold? An approved medicine, a compounded product, a supplement, a telehealth subscription or a course?
  3. How does the creator benefit? Payment, code, affiliate link, free product, ownership?
  4. Are risks mentioned at all? Nausea, gallbladder problems, pancreatitis and other warnings are on every GLP-1 label.
  5. Do the claims match the label? "No side effects," "works for everyone," and specific weight-loss promises do not appear in any label. (How to check current prescribing information.)
  6. Is urgency doing the selling? "Price goes up tonight," "before it's banned" and "limited spots" are pressure, not information.

If a post fails three or more, enjoy it as entertainment and get your medical information somewhere else.

Credentials do not cancel conflicts

A "Dr." in a username tells you about training, not about who pays. Researchers in journals have to declare conflicts of interest; we explain how to read those in conflicts of interest in GLP-1 research. Hold health creators to a similar standard: who funds you, and how much does that relationship shape what you say?

Equally, a patient creator's story can be honest and moving and still be one person's experience. One person's result is not an average, and it cannot show safety. Our guide on red flags in viral GLP-1 tips covers the content side; this post is about the money side.

What to do with a sponsored post you found helpful

  • Keep the useful part. A good protein recipe or injection tip is still good.
  • Check the medical part against the label or with your pharmacist.
  • Buy through your own channels. Use your own prescriber and a state-licensed pharmacy, not a code that routes you to an unknown seller.
  • Report misleading drug promotion. FDA's Bad Ad Program is aimed mainly at health care providers, but anyone can report potentially false or misleading prescription drug promotion to BadAd@fda.gov or 855-RX-BADAD (855-792-2323).

And if you are tracking your own GLP-1 journey, remember that your logs are the most relevant "review" you will ever read. Weight trend, doses and side effects over months, whether in GLPily or a notebook, tell you more about how a medicine is working for you than any creator can.

Common questions

Do medical influencers have to disclose sponsorships?

Under FTC guidance, anyone endorsing a product must clearly disclose material connections to the brand, such as payment, free products or employment. Doctors are not exempt.

Is "#ad" at the end of a long caption enough?

The FTC says disclosures should be hard to miss and not buried among hashtags or behind a "more" link. In videos, the disclosure should be in the video itself.

Does an affiliate link count as a sponsorship?

It is a financial relationship: the creator earns when you buy. The FTC treats that kind of connection as something that should be disclosed.

Can influencers promote prescription GLP-1 medicines?

Drug companies' promotion, including through paid influencers, falls under FDA rules that require balanced risk information. FDA's 2025 crackdown specifically named undisclosed paid influencer promotion as a concern.
