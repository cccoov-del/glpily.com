15:T11b9,<p>To evaluate a medical influencer&#39;s GLP-1 sponsorship disclosure, check whether the relationship is stated clearly and early in the content itself (for example &quot;ad,&quot; &quot;sponsored&quot; or &quot;paid partnership with [brand]&quot;), what kind of benefit the creator gets (payment, free product, affiliate commission, discount code, equity or a job), and whether the health claims match the official label. Under FTC guidance, a disclosure hidden in a bio, buried after &quot;more,&quot; or written as &quot;collab&quot; is not good enough.</p>
<p>Sponsorship does not make someone wrong. Doctors, dietitians and patients with sponsors can share useful, accurate information. But money is context, and with prescription medicines and telehealth offers, context matters a lot.</p>
<h2 id="what-do-u-s-rules-say-influencers-must-disclose">What do U.S. rules say influencers must disclose?</h2>
<p>The FTC&#39;s &quot;Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers&quot; is short and surprisingly readable. The core points:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Disclose any financial, employment, personal or family relationship</strong> with a brand. That includes free or discounted products. Compensation does not have to be money.</li>
<li><strong>Disclose even if you think you are unbiased</strong>, and even if the brand did not ask you to mention that specific product.</li>
<li><strong>Put it where people will see it.</strong> Not only on a profile page, at the end of a post, or behind a &quot;more&quot; link.</li>
<li><strong>In videos, say it in the video</strong>, not just in the description. In live streams, repeat it.</li>
<li><strong>Use plain words</strong> like &quot;ad,&quot; &quot;sponsored,&quot; or &quot;thanks to [brand] for the free product.&quot; Vague tags like &quot;sp,&quot; &quot;collab&quot; or &quot;thanks&quot; do not cut it.</li>
<li><strong>Do not rely only on a platform&#39;s built-in disclosure tool.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Do not make health claims you cannot back up</strong> with proper scientific support.</li>
</ul>
<p>Since 2024, the FTC&#39;s rule on fake reviews and testimonials also bans AI-generated or otherwise fake testimonials, undisclosed reviews from company insiders, and buying fake followers or views to misrepresent influence, with civil penalties for knowing violations.</p>
<h2 id="why-are-glp-1-posts-under-extra-scrutiny">Why are GLP-1 posts under extra scrutiny?</h2>
<p>Because prescription drug promotion has its own rules. On September 9, 2025, FDA announced a crackdown on deceptive drug advertising, sending thousands of letters to drug companies and about 100 cease-and-desist letters. FDA&#39;s announcement singled out undisclosed paid influencer promotion and social media for blurring the line between editorial content, user posts and drug ads. It also cited a 2024 review finding that every pharmaceutical social media post studied highlighted benefits, but only 33% mentioned potential harms.</p>
<p>That 33% figure is a useful mental check. If a GLP-1 post is all upside, the missing half is probably not an accident. (For what FDA has said specifically to telehealth sellers, see <a href="/blog/fda-telehealth-glp-1-warning-letters-2026/">FDA warning letters to telehealth companies</a>.)</p>

1c:T113a,<h2 id="a-6-point-money-trail-check">A 6-point money-trail check</h2>
<p>Run this in a minute before acting on a GLP-1 post:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Is there a clear disclosure in the first seconds or first lines?</strong> If you had to dig, note that.</li>
<li><strong>What exactly is being sold?</strong> An approved medicine, a compounded product, a supplement, a telehealth subscription or a course?</li>
<li><strong>How does the creator benefit?</strong> Payment, code, affiliate link, free product, ownership?</li>
<li><strong>Are risks mentioned at all?</strong> Nausea, gallbladder problems, pancreatitis and other warnings are on every GLP-1 label.</li>
<li><strong>Do the claims match the label?</strong> &quot;No side effects,&quot; &quot;works for everyone,&quot; and specific weight-loss promises do not appear in any label. (<a href="/blog/check-current-glp-1-prescribing-information/">How to check current prescribing information</a>.)</li>
<li><strong>Is urgency doing the selling?</strong> &quot;Price goes up tonight,&quot; &quot;before it&#39;s banned&quot; and &quot;limited spots&quot; are pressure, not information.</li>
</ol>
<p>If a post fails three or more, enjoy it as entertainment and get your medical information somewhere else.</p>
<h2 id="credentials-do-not-cancel-conflicts">Credentials do not cancel conflicts</h2>
<p>A &quot;Dr.&quot; in a username tells you about training, not about who pays. Researchers in journals have to declare conflicts of interest; we explain how to read those in <a href="/blog/conflicts-interest-glp-1-research-disclosure/">conflicts of interest in GLP-1 research</a>. Hold health creators to a similar standard: who funds you, and how much does that relationship shape what you say?</p>
<p>Equally, a patient creator&#39;s story can be honest and moving and still be one person&#39;s experience. One person&#39;s result is not an average, and it cannot show safety. Our guide on <a href="/blog/glp-1-social-media-misinformation/">red flags in viral GLP-1 tips</a> covers the content side; this post is about the money side.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-do-with-a-sponsored-post-you-found-helpful">What to do with a sponsored post you found helpful</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Keep the useful part.</strong> A good protein recipe or injection tip is still good.</li>
<li><strong>Check the medical part</strong> against the label or with your pharmacist.</li>
<li><strong>Buy through your own channels.</strong> Use your own prescriber and a state-licensed pharmacy, not a code that routes you to an unknown seller.</li>
<li><strong>Report misleading drug promotion.</strong> FDA&#39;s Bad Ad Program is aimed mainly at health care providers, but anyone can report potentially false or misleading prescription drug promotion to <a href="mailto:BadAd@fda.gov">BadAd@fda.gov</a> or 855-RX-BADAD (855-792-2323).</li>
</ul>
<p>And if you are tracking your own GLP-1 journey, remember that your logs are the most relevant &quot;review&quot; you will ever read. Weight trend, doses and side effects over months, whether in GLPily or a notebook, tell you more about how a medicine is working for you than any creator can.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="do-medical-influencers-have-to-disclose-sponsorships">Do medical influencers have to disclose sponsorships?</h3>
<p>Under FTC guidance, anyone endorsing a product must clearly disclose material connections to the brand, such as payment, free products or employment. Doctors are not exempt.</p>
<h3 id="is-ad-at-the-end-of-a-long-caption-enough">Is &quot;#ad&quot; at the end of a long caption enough?</h3>
<p>The FTC says disclosures should be hard to miss and not buried among hashtags or behind a &quot;more&quot; link. In videos, the disclosure should be in the video itself.</p>
<h3 id="does-an-affiliate-link-count-as-a-sponsorship">Does an affiliate link count as a sponsorship?</h3>
<p>It is a financial relationship: the creator earns when you buy. The FTC treats that kind of connection as something that should be disclosed.</p>
<h3 id="can-influencers-promote-prescription-glp-1-medicines">Can influencers promote prescription GLP-1 medicines?</h3>
