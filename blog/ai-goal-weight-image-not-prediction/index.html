AI Goal-Weight Images: Fun Inspiration, Not a Body Forecast

An AI picture of 'future you' is a drawing made by a model that has never met your skin, muscles or genes. Here is what it can be good for, and why trials show bodies change in ways no image can predict.

An AI goal-weight image is a picture a generative model invents from your photo and a target number. It can be motivating or just fun, but it is not a prediction: the model does not know how your fat, muscle, skin, face or posture will actually change, and it tends to draw an idealized body rather than a realistic one.

We make GLPily, which includes a "Your New You" goal-weight image, so we have thought about this more than most. Here is the honest version.

What is an AI goal-weight image actually doing?

A generative image model is very good at producing a plausible-looking picture. Give it your photo and an instruction like "this person at a lower weight," and it produces an image that looks like a believable photo of someone similar to you.

What it is not doing:

  • Measuring your body composition.
  • Modeling where your body tends to store or lose fat.
  • Knowing your age, skin elasticity, muscle mass, medication, or how long you will be on it.
  • Checking whether the target weight is a healthy or realistic goal for you.

Even when a tool tries hard to keep your identity, image models can quietly change things: jawline, hair, skin texture, proportions, even the lighting that makes you look rested. A polished result is a sign the model is good at pictures, not that the picture is medically realistic.

Why can't an AI image predict your future body?

Because real weight loss is uneven, and averages hide huge differences between people.

Same medicine, very different results. In the main obesity trial behind Wegovy (STEP 1, 68 weeks), the label reports an average weight change of -14.9% with the medicine vs -2.4% with placebo. But look at the spread among people on the medicine:

Weight lost at week 68 Share of people on Wegovy 2.4 mg
At least 5% 83.5%
At least 10% 66.1%
At least 15% 47.9%

So roughly half reached 15%, and about one in six did not reach 5%. An image tool generating "you at your goal" cannot know which of those groups you will be in. (More on reading these numbers: Average Weight Loss vs People Reaching a Target.)

Weight is not one substance. In a small body-composition substudy of STEP 1 (140 people scanned by DXA), people on semaglutide lost about 19% of their fat mass and about 27% of visceral fat, and lean mass also fell by about 10%. The proportion of lean mass in the body went up, but the mix of what was lost varied. An AI image has no idea how much of your change will be fat, muscle or water. See Hair Loss and Muscle Loss on GLP-1s for why that matters.

Skin and face change on their own schedule. Loose skin, facial volume and posture all respond differently from person to person, and on their own timelines.

Do AI image generators have a "body type" bias?

Research suggests yes. A University of Toronto team published a study in Psychology of Popular Media (reported November 2025) in which they asked three popular image generators (Midjourney, DALL-E and Stable Diffusion) to create bodies, including athletes, and coded 300 resulting images. The images skewed young, white and lean, with very low body fat and defined muscles, especially for athletes. No images showed visible disabilities.

That matters for goal-weight images. A model trained on idealized pictures can nudge "future you" toward an ideal, not toward you. If the image makes you feel worse about your current body rather than hopeful, that is the model's bias talking.

So what is an AI goal-weight image good for?

Plenty, if you use it the way you would use a vision board:

  • A spark of motivation on a hard week. Some people like having a hopeful picture to look at.
  • Imagining a feeling, not a measurement. "I want to feel comfortable hiking again" is a better takeaway than "I must look exactly like that."
  • Playing with style. Clothes, colors, a haircut you have been considering.
  • A conversation starter with yourself about what you actually want from treatment: energy, mobility, lab results, comfort.

When should you skip it?

Skip it, or close it, if:

  • It pulls you toward a weight your clinician has not agreed is appropriate.
  • You find yourself comparing your real mirror to the generated picture every morning.
  • You have a history of disordered eating and images of bodies tend to be triggering. (GLP-1 Medicines and Eating-Disorder History is worth reading.)
  • It is making the scale feel like a test you are failing.

None of these mean you did something wrong. They mean the tool is not doing its job for you.

Can you share an AI goal-weight image online?

You can, but label it. Posting a generated image next to a real "before" photo as if it were your result is misleading, even as a joke, because people scroll fast. The FTC's guidance on endorsements already says that when an ad shows someone's above-average weight-loss result, it must make clear what people can generally expect. You are not an advertiser, but the same honesty principle makes the internet a kinder place. See Why Before-and-After GLP-1 Stories Do Not Tell the Whole Story.

Privacy check before you upload a body photo

A goal image starts with a real photo of you, often a sensitive one. Before uploading, check where the image is processed, how long it is kept, whether it is used to train AI, and how to delete it. Then check your phone, too: the generated picture may land in a photo library that syncs to a shared account. If that is a concern, Using a GLP-1 App on a Shared Phone walks through the settings.

How we think about "Your New You"

Since we offer one of these features, here is our own rule: the picture is an illustration for inspiration, not a forecast of your body or a promise about results. Your real progress lives in your actual logs, measurements and progress photos, and your goal weight is something to set with your clinician.

Common questions

Is an AI goal-weight image accurate?

No image model can know how your body will change. It produces a plausible picture, not a prediction, and different tools will draw very different versions of "you."

Can AI predict what I will look like after weight loss on a GLP-1?

Not reliably. Trials show wide variation in how much weight people lose and what kind of tissue they lose, and skin and face changes vary too. An image can only guess.

Can AI goal-weight images be bad for body image?

They can for some people, especially if the model leans toward idealized bodies. If the image leaves you feeling worse, stop using it and focus on measures that reflect your real progress.

Is it OK to post an AI weight-loss image on social media?

Only if you clearly say it is AI-generated. Presenting it as a real result can mislead others who are comparing themselves to it.
