Mounjaro vs Ozempic: What’s Actually Different?

Compare Mounjaro vs Ozempic: active ingredients, US-approved uses, weight-loss evidence, side effects and questions to ask your prescriber.

Search “Mounjaro vs Ozempic” and you'll find confident winner-picking everywhere. The real answer is more nuanced: these are different medicines with different active ingredients, product labels and evidence. Which one is appropriate depends on a person's health, treatment goal, medical history, tolerability and local access.

First, the names can be confusing

Mounjaro contains tirzepatide. In the United States, Mounjaro is approved for blood-sugar control in adults with type 2 diabetes and, since August 2026, to lower the risk of major cardiovascular events in adults with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk. In the United States, its label covers blood-sugar control in adults with type 2 diabetes. Tirzepatide is also marketed as Zepbound for chronic weight management in eligible adults, and Zepbound has a separate US indication for certain adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea.

Ozempic contains semaglutide and is a type 2 diabetes medicine in the United States. Wegovy also contains semaglutide, but has its own weight-management label and other specific approved uses. The same active ingredient does not make the product brands, dosing instructions, devices or indications interchangeable.

Approvals differ by country and change over time, so the current local product label matters.

How are they different?

Semaglutide activates the GLP-1 receptor. Tirzepatide activates both GIP and GLP-1 receptors. Both affect pathways involved in blood glucose regulation and appetite, but they are not the same molecule. A description of how a drug works does not determine how effective or tolerable it will be for one particular person.

What do weight-loss studies show?

There is now a direct trial that helps answer the weight-loss comparison more fairly. In SURMOUNT-5, a 72-week, open-label randomized trial in 751 adults with obesity who did not have type 2 diabetes, participants received maximum tolerated tirzepatide (10 mg or 15 mg) or semaglutide (1.7 mg or 2.4 mg). Average body-weight change at week 72 was −20.2% with tirzepatide and −13.7% with semaglutide. The trial was funded by Eli Lilly, the manufacturer of tirzepatide; the funding and open-label design belong in the context, not hidden in the fine print.

Those are group averages from a specific study, not a guarantee for an individual. The participants did not have type 2 diabetes, and the trial compared obesity-treatment doses, not every product, dose, or use people might mean when they say “Mounjaro vs Ozempic.” SURPASS-2 separately compared tirzepatide with semaglutide 1 mg in adults with type 2 diabetes, so it answers a different question.

A better question is which locally approved product fits the person's health needs, what side effects and costs matter, and how the prescriber will monitor treatment over time.

What about side effects?

Digestive effects are common with both medicines. Depending on the product, these can include nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, constipation, abdominal discomfort and indigestion. Frequency and severity vary, and not everyone experiences the same symptoms.

Both medicines have important warnings and situations where they should not be used. Don't treat severe or persistent symptoms as proof that a medicine is “working better.” Use the current patient information and ask the prescriber what to monitor and when to seek advice.

Are there benefits beyond weight loss?

Evidence is specific to the drug and population studied. Wegovy has a US indication to reduce certain major cardiovascular events in adults with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity. Zepbound has a US indication for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. Those findings don't mean every GLP-1 medicine has the same approved benefits, or that either drug is appropriate for everyone.

Within type 2 diabetes, Ozempic's US label also includes reducing major cardiovascular events in adults with established heart disease, and reducing kidney-disease worsening and cardiovascular death in adults with chronic kidney disease. Mounjaro's label includes a cardiovascular risk-reduction indication based on a trial against another GLP-1 medicine, dulaglutide.

Cost, access and the actual product matter

Coverage, availability, supply, local approvals and out-of-pocket cost can affect treatment in practice. Be cautious with unapproved online products or sellers marketing “research peptides.” The FDA has warned about concerns involving unapproved GLP-1 products. Ask a clinician or pharmacist about a specific product rather than relying on a social-media recommendation.

Questions to take to your prescriber

  • What is the main goal of treatment in my case?
  • Which product and indication apply in my country?
  • What side effects should I know about, and which symptoms need prompt attention?
  • How will we evaluate whether treatment is helping?
  • What should I do if side effects, cost or supply become a problem?
  • Could the medicine interact with another medicine or a planned procedure?

So, which is better?

There isn't one universally best option. The medicines differ, and studies are not always directly comparable. The decision belongs with the person and their qualified prescriber, using current evidence and individual circumstances.
