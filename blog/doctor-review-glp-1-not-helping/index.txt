19:Tc53,<p>If a GLP-1 medicine isn&#39;t delivering the change you expected, the most useful next step is a structured review, not guessing at a stronger dose. A clinician first needs to understand the exact product, why it was prescribed, how long you&#39;ve been on the prescribed plan, the effect so far, side effects and any gaps in access. Those details help separate an early expectation mismatch from a more persistent low response.</p>
<h2 id="review-the-timeline-and-the-outcome">Review the timeline and the outcome</h2>
<p>A few weeks into a dose-escalation schedule is very different from months on a maintenance dose. Many weekly GLP-1 medicines start low and step up over several months, partly to reduce digestive side effects, so early weeks aren&#39;t a fair test of the full effect. Weight loss also isn&#39;t the only possible goal for someone taking a diabetes medicine. A clinician compares the relevant outcome with the timeframe and criteria for that treatment, rather than someone else&#39;s social-media timeline.</p>
<h2 id="review-the-whole-medication-and-health-picture">Review the whole medication and health picture</h2>
<p>Other medicines, underlying conditions, sleep, mobility, mental health, nutrition, cost and access can all influence results. Some commonly prescribed medicines, including insulin, sulfonylureas and certain antidepressants, antipsychotics and steroids, are associated with weight gain, which is one reason a full medication list matters. A 2026 systematic review of low weight-loss response to incretin medicines found associations with factors such as higher starting weight, higher HbA1c, insulin resistance, older age, male sex and shorter or less consistent treatment. Those associations can&#39;t tell a clinician which factor applies to you without an individual assessment. Sometimes side effects lead to low intake or missed doses; sometimes the goal or the strategy needs a rethink.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-bring">What to bring</h2>
<p>Bring a current medication list, treatment start date, any interruptions, symptom notes and a simple weight trend if you track one. If you&#39;re not sure what&#39;s worth noting, here&#39;s <a href="/blog/what-to-track-on-glp-1/">a practical list of what to track on a GLP-1</a>. If the main goal is blood-sugar control, bring the readings or lab results your care team asked you to monitor. Be candid about cost or missed doses. These are practical barriers, not personal failures.</p>
<h2 id="questions-to-ask">Questions to ask</h2>
<ul>
<li>Are we evaluating the right outcome for this medicine?</li>
<li>Has the treatment been used long enough to judge, according to its dosing plan?</li>
<li>Could another condition or medicine be affecting the result?</li>
<li>What would make us continue, adjust, change or stop the plan, and when should we review again?</li>
</ul>
<p>Only your clinician can decide whether a medication change is appropriate. A clear review makes that decision more evidence-based and less reactive. If you&#39;re approaching that point, a <a href="/blog/three-month-glp-1-check-in/">three-month check-in</a> is a good moment to raise it.</p>
