15:T16a0,<p>Milligrams (mg), milliliters (mL) and &quot;units&quot; are not three names for the same thing. A milligram is how much <strong>drug</strong> you get, a milliliter is how much <strong>liquid</strong> you inject, and a &quot;unit&quot; is a <strong>line on a syringe</strong> that measures liquid. The only thing that links them is the concentration of your specific vial, which is why converting between them from a forum post or a chart for someone else&#39;s vial is how real overdoses happen.</p>
<p>That is not a hypothetical. The FDA has published reports of people with compounded semaglutide injecting <strong>5 to 20 times</strong> their intended dose because of exactly this mix-up. Let&#39;s untangle the three words so you know which questions to ask, and who should answer them.</p>
<h2 id="what-does-each-unit-actually-measure">What does each unit actually measure?</h2>
<p>Think of a coffee order: &quot;two shots&quot; is how much coffee, &quot;12 ounces&quot; is how much liquid. You can&#39;t get from one to the other without knowing how strong the coffee is.</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Term</th>
<th>What it measures</th>

<th>Example</th>
</tr>
</thead>

<td><strong>mg (milligram)</strong></td>
<td>Mass of the active drug</td>
<td>Your prescription, the label, the pen dose window</td>
<td>&quot;0.25 mg once weekly&quot;</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>mL (milliliter)</strong></td>
<td>Volume of liquid</td>

<p>Your <strong>dose</strong> is always a drug amount in mg. mL and units only describe the vehicle that carries it.</p>
<h2 id="why-units-cause-the-most-confusion">Why &quot;units&quot; cause the most confusion</h2>
<p>&quot;Units&quot; were made for insulin, where the drug itself is measured in units. GLP-1 medicines are not. When a compounded GLP-1 is drawn up in an insulin syringe, the &quot;units&quot; on the barrel are just volume markings. On a U-100 syringe, each unit is one hundredth of a mL.</p>
<p>So &quot;draw up 20 units&quot; means 0.2 mL of liquid. How many milligrams of semaglutide or tirzepatide that is depends entirely on the vial&#39;s concentration. Two vials from two pharmacies can have different strengths, and the same 20 units can then contain very different doses.</p>
<p>The FDA&#39;s July 2024 alert on compounded semaglutide dosing errors lists exactly how this goes wrong:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Patients drawing too much.</strong> Most reports involved people pulling more liquid than prescribed from a multi-dose vial. In one example, a dose meant to be <strong>5 units (0.05 mL)</strong> was taken as <strong>50 units</strong>. That is a tenfold difference that looks like &quot;one zero&quot; on paper.</li>
<li><strong>Professionals miscalculating.</strong> Converting mg into units or mL caused 5x to 10x errors. In one report an intended 0.25 mg (5 units) was prescribed as 25 units, and the person had severe vomiting. In another, three patients received 20 units instead of 2.</li>
<li><strong>Giant syringes.</strong> Some people were sent syringes much larger than the volume they needed. A 1 mL syringe for a 0.05 mL dose leaves a lot of room for error.</li>
<li><strong>Searching online.</strong> One telehealth patient could not get clear instructions, looked it up online, and took five times the intended dose.</li>
</ul>
<p>Reported effects included nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fainting, headache, dehydration, pancreatitis and gallstones, and some people were hospitalized. As of May 31, 2026, the FDA said it had received about 990 adverse event reports linked to compounded semaglutide and more than 730 linked to compounded tirzepatide. Not all of those are dosing errors, and reports alone do not prove the drug caused each event, but the FDA calls them likely underreported.</p>

<p>Even FDA-approved products show why volume is not dose. The current Zepbound label (updated August 2026) lists multi-dose vials and KwikPens that each hold four weekly doses. Every vial strength gives a dose of <strong>0.6 mL</strong>, but what is in that 0.6 mL changes:</p>

So &quot;I always inject 0.5&quot; or &quot;I always do 60 units&quot; means nothing unless you know which product it applies to.</p>
<p>Ozempic pens follow the same logic: the label lists a 2 mg/3 mL pen (0.68 mg/mL) and an 8 mg/3 mL pen (2.68 mg/mL). The dial shows mg so you never have to do this math. That is the whole point of a pen.</p>

1c:T129d,<h2 id="why-one-number-from-the-internet-is-dangerous">Why one number from the internet is dangerous</h2>
<p>A post saying &quot;I take 25 units&quot; carries three hidden variables: which drug, which concentration, which syringe. Copy the number without all three and you might get a fraction of what you need or a multiple of it. It also comes up a lot in <a href="/blog/glp-1-microdosing-trend-evidence/">microdosing posts</a>, where people trade &quot;unit&quot; amounts across products that were never the same.</p>
<p>Common traps:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Switching pharmacies or vial strengths</strong> and reusing your old unit number.</li>
<li><strong>Using a different syringe</strong> than the one your instructions were written for (a 0.3 mL, 0.5 mL and 1 mL syringe space their lines differently).</li>
<li><strong>Reading &quot;0.25&quot; as mL when it was mg</strong>, or the reverse.</li>
<li><strong>Online &quot;unit converters&quot;</strong> that assume a concentration you do not have.</li>
<li><strong>Dropping or adding a decimal point</strong> (0.05 vs 0.5).</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="how-to-get-it-right-instead">How to get it right instead</h2>
<p>You do not need to become a pharmacist. You need one, plus a few questions.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Ask for your dose in mg <em>and</em> in the exact volume or syringe marking.</strong> Then ask the pharmacist to say it back to you on your actual syringe, not a picture.</li>
<li><strong>Ask to be shown.</strong> The FDA tells patients to talk with their provider or compounder about how to measure and inject the intended dose. A demonstration with your own vial beats any video.</li>
<li><strong>Check the concentration every refill.</strong> If the mg/mL on the new vial is different, your volume may change. Call before your next dose.</li>
<li><strong>Ask for a syringe sized for your dose.</strong> The FDA asks providers to give out syringes that match the intended volume.</li>
<li><strong>Follow the vial&#39;s discard date.</strong> FDA guidance says to throw away a multi-dose vial 28 days after first use, even if liquid remains.</li>
<li><strong>Write it down, in both forms.</strong> Logging your dose in mg (with the product name) makes changes easy to spot. In GLPily you log each shot with its dose and get reminders, which helps you notice when a refill or new strength changes things.</li>
</ol>
<p>If the explanation you got still does not make sense, that is a sign to ask again, not to guess. Pharmacists get this question all the time. For a broader look at quality concerns with compounded products, see <a href="/blog/compounded-glp-1-safety-red-flags/">our compounded GLP-1 safety guide</a>, and <a href="/blog/check-legitimate-online-pharmacy-glp-1/">how to check whether an online pharmacy is legitimate</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-if-you-think-you-already-measured-wrong">What if you think you already measured wrong?</h2>
<p>Do not try to &quot;even it out&quot; by skipping or splitting the next dose on your own. Call the Poison Help line at <strong>1-800-222-1222</strong> (free and confidential), your pharmacist or your prescriber. Call <strong>911</strong> for trouble breathing, fainting, seizures or someone who cannot be woken. The labels for semaglutide and tirzepatide note long half-lives (about one week and about five days), which is why effects can last and why observation may be needed. Our step-by-step guide covers <a href="/blog/wrong-dose-glp-1-what-to-do/">what to do if you took the wrong amount</a>.</p>
<h2 id="common-questions">Common questions</h2>
<h3 id="how-many-units-is-0-25-mg-of-semaglutide">How many units is 0.25 mg of semaglutide?</h3>
<p>There is no single answer, because it depends on the concentration of your vial and the syringe you use. That is exactly why the FDA has seen conversion errors. Ask your pharmacist to show your dose on your own syringe.</p>
<h3 id="is-1-unit-the-same-as-1-mg">Is 1 unit the same as 1 mg?</h3>
<p>No. On a U-100 insulin syringe, 1 unit is 0.01 mL of liquid. How many mg are in that liquid depends on the mg/mL printed on your vial.</p>
<h3 id="do-branded-pens-have-this-problem">Do branded pens have this problem?</h3>
<p>Pens like Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro and Zepbound are set up to deliver a dose in mg, so you don&#39;t convert anything. The confusion mostly comes with vials and syringes, especially compounded ones with varying concentrations. Vials still need careful instruction.</p>
<h3 id="can-i-use-an-online-glp-1-units-calculator">Can I use an online GLP-1 units calculator?</h3>
<p>It&#39;s safer not to. A calculator only works if you enter the right concentration and syringe, and it can&#39;t catch a typo. Let your pharmacist or prescriber confirm the volume for your exact product.</p>
