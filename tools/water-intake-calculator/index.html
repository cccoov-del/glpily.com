How it’s calculated

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) considers 2.0 liters a day adequate for women and 2.5 liters for men. These are amounts of total water — what you drink plus the water in food. The US National Academies estimate that about 80% of total water typically comes from drinks (including caffeinated ones) and about 20% from food. So:

From drinks ≈ reference × 0.8 Glasses = from drinks ÷ glass size Rule of thumb (rough) = body weight (kg) × 30–35 ml

Worked example: for a woman, 2.0 L × 0.8 = 1.6 L from drinks — about 54 fl oz, or roughly seven 8-oz glasses. The other 0.4 L or so comes from food such as fruit, vegetables, soup and yogurt.

The US figures describe the average total intake of people who appear adequately hydrated, which is one reason they’re higher than EFSA’s. Both include water from food.

About the 30–35 ml per kg rule

Multiplying body weight by 30–35 ml is a popular shortcut, but it isn’t an official reference value. At higher body weights it gives numbers well above the references — 120 kg (265 lb) works out to 3.6–4.2 L — which doesn’t mean you need that much. Treat it as a rough cross-check only.

When you might need more — or less

  • More in hot weather, with exercise, and when you’re ill with fever, vomiting or diarrhea.
  • More during pregnancy and breastfeeding — ask your midwife or clinician.
  • Some heart and kidney conditions call for a fluid limit. If you’ve been given one, follow it.

Some people find they drink less when their appetite is smaller — for example while taking an appetite-reducing medication — and like a simple reminder: a bottle within sight, a glass with every meal, or ticking off glasses as they go.

Frequently asked questions

How much water should I drink a day?

For healthy adults, EFSA considers 2.0 L (women) and 2.5 L (men) of total water a day adequate. Since about a fifth usually comes from food, that’s roughly 1.6–2.0 L to drink — more in heat, with exercise or when you’re unwell.

Do coffee, tea and other drinks count?

Yes. The US National Academies concluded that caffeinated drinks can contribute to daily hydration as much as non-caffeinated ones, and milk, juice and soup count too.

Is “8 glasses a day” right?

It’s a rule of thumb rather than a reference value, but it’s in the same ballpark: 1.6–2.0 L to drink is about 7 to 8½ glasses of 8 oz.

Why are the US numbers higher than EFSA’s?

They’re defined differently: the US values are the average total intake of adequately hydrated people, while EFSA sets an amount it considers adequate. Both are for healthy adults and include water from food.

I forget to drink during the day. Any tips?

Keep a bottle where you can see it, pair a glass with routines like meals or coffee, and track glasses so you can see how the day is going. The US National Academies note that most people meet their needs through normal drinking and by letting thirst guide them.
